A dead toy, a binned greeting card, a stripped-out PC — each one is hiding a tiny voice you can bring back to life. In this project you will rescue a buzzer or a small speaker from the e-waste pile and teach your Raspberry Pi 5 to sing: first a clean musical scale, then a recognisable tune. Along the way you will learn three of Python's most useful ideas — dictionaries, functions, and loops — and the electronics behind sound: PWM tone generation, the difference between an active and a passive buzzer, and the all-important rule that a real speaker must never be wired straight to a GPIO pin.

What You'll Build & Learn

By the end of this chapter your Pi will play music through a salvaged sounder. You will build the project in three stages: a single test beep, a full eight-note scale, and finally a short melody stored as data and played back by a loop.

The Python fundamentals

  • Dictionaries — a way to store pairs of values, like a real dictionary maps a word to its meaning. Here we map each note name ("C4", "D4"…) to its frequency in hertz.
  • Functions — named, reusable blocks of code. We will write a play_note() function once and call it many times.
  • Loops — repeating actions without copying and pasting. A for loop will step through a scale and through a melody.
  • A list of tuples — storing a melody as an ordered list where each item is a small bundle of two values: a note name and how long to hold it.

The electronics fundamentals

  • PWM and tone generation — how the Pi makes a pitch by switching a pin on and off thousands of times a second.
  • Active vs passive buzzers — why one type only ever makes a single fixed beep, while the other can play any note.
  • gpiozero TonalBuzzer and Tone — the friendly objects that turn a note name into a sound.
  • Driving a speaker safely — why a small loudspeaker needs an NPN transistor driver and must never be connected directly to a GPIO pin.
Note: Everything here runs on a Raspberry Pi 5 with Raspberry Pi OS Bookworm. The gpiozero library and the Thonny editor are already installed, and on the Pi 5 gpiozero quietly uses the lgpio backend. There is nothing to download to get started.

How Sound Actually Works

Sound is nothing more than air being pushed and pulled very quickly. If you can make something vibrate back and forth — a speaker cone, a thin metal disc — it shoves the surrounding air into waves, and your ears hear those waves as a tone. The faster the vibration, the higher the pitch.

In plain words: Frequency is how many times something vibrates per second, measured in hertz (Hz). A vibration of 440 times a second (440 Hz) is the musical note A above middle C — the note an orchestra tunes to. Double the frequency and you go up one octave: 880 Hz is the A one octave higher.

So to play a tune, all the Pi has to do is make a sounder vibrate at the right frequency for each note. Here are the frequencies for one octave of natural notes, which we will use throughout the chapter:

Active vs Passive Buzzers

Before wiring anything, you need to know which kind of buzzer you have, because they behave very differently — and the code is different for each.

An active buzzer

An active buzzer has its own little oscillator circuit built in. Give it power and it makes a sound at one fixed pitch — that is all it can do. You cannot play a tune on it; you can only switch its single beep on and off. It is perfect for an alarm or a "ping", useless for music.

A passive buzzer

A passive buzzer (also called a piezo transducer) has no oscillator inside. It is silent until you feed it a rapidly switching signal — and the speed at which you switch it decides the pitch. Because you control the frequency, a passive buzzer can play any note, which is exactly what we want for a melody.

In plain words: "Active" means the buzzer is active on its own — it generates its own tone. "Passive" means it just sits there passively until your Pi drives it with a changing signal. Counter-intuitively, the passive one is the clever musical one.

How to tell them apart

  • By printing on the case: some are marked. Two-leg sounders are often unmarked, though.
  • By the legs: an active buzzer usually has one longer leg (the +) and may have a sticker over the sound hole. A passive piezo disc often has two solder pads or two thin wires (red and black) with no polarity fuss.
  • The battery test (the reliable one): briefly touch the buzzer's two legs to a 3 V coin cell (longer leg / red wire to +). If you hear a clear, steady tone, it is active. If you hear only a faint click, or nothing, it is passive — it needs a switching signal, which the battery cannot provide.
Note: This chapter focuses on the passive buzzer (and, later, a salvaged speaker) because those are the ones that can make music. If all you have is an active buzzer, you can still follow along with the Buzzer object to make rhythmic beeps — see "Make it your own" at the end.

You almost certainly own a dead sounder already. Here is where to find one and how to harvest it safely.

Careful: Only ever harvest from low-voltage, battery-powered e-waste. Never open a microwave oven, a CRT (tube) TV or monitor, or any mains adapter — these can hold a lethal charge in their capacitors even when unplugged. Never cut or crush a lithium battery; it can catch fire. If a device plugs into the wall, leave its insides alone and salvage only the obviously low-voltage parts (like a clip-on speaker) after the mains section has been removed by an adult who knows what they are doing.

Harvesting it

  1. Free the sounder. Snip the two wires close to the old circuit board, leaving as much wire on the buzzer as you can. If it is soldered to pads, a gentle pull with pliers usually frees it.
  2. Tidy the wire ends. Strip about 5 mm of insulation off each wire. If the buzzer has bare metal legs, leave them as they are.
  3. Test it with the coin-cell test above to decide active or passive.
  4. Tin or fit a header. To plug into a breadboard, solder a stiff jumper pin onto each wire, or twist each wire around a male jumper and tape it. Stiff legs push into breadboard holes more reliably than floppy wire.

PWM: Making a Pitch from a Pin

A GPIO pin can only ever be in one of two states: HIGH (3.3 V) or LOW (0 V). How can two states make a smooth musical note? The trick is speed.

In plain words: PWM stands for Pulse-Width Modulation. The pin is switched on and off very fast, over and over. The number of complete on-off cycles per second is the frequency. If the pin cycles 440 times a second, the buzzer's disc is pushed and pulled 440 times a second — and you hear the note A4.

So "playing a note" really means "switch this pin on and off at the note's frequency". To play A4 we cycle at 440 Hz; to play C5 we cycle at 523 Hz. Happily, gpiozero hides all the timing for us: we just tell it the note and it sets the right PWM frequency.

Meet the TonalBuzzer Object

gpiozero gives us two ready-made objects for sounders:

  • Buzzer(pin) — for an active buzzer. It can only do on(), off() and beep().
  • TonalBuzzer(pin) — for a passive buzzer. It can play() a Tone (a musical note) and stop().

A Tone is gpiozero's way of describing a pitch. Beautifully, you can create the same tone three ways — they are all equal:

from gpiozero.tones import Tone

Tone("A4")    # by note name
Tone(440.0)   # by frequency in hertz
Tone(69)      # by MIDI number

Line 1 — from gpiozero.tones import Tone: The Tone class lives in a sub-module of gpiozero called tones. This line fetches it so we can describe musical notes.

Line 3 — Tone("A4"): Creates the tone for the note A4 by its musical name. This is the friendliest form and the one we will use.

Line 4 — Tone(440.0): The identical tone, described instead by its frequency, 440 Hz. Note the decimal point — a float — which tells gpiozero you mean hertz, not a MIDI number.

Line 5 — Tone(69): The same tone again, given as a plain whole number, which gpiozero reads as a MIDI note number (69 is A4). All three lines describe exactly the same pitch.

Note: TonalBuzzer plays a limited range of octaves around a middle tone (by default one octave either side of A4). The eight notes C4–C5 we are using sit comfortably inside that range.

A Tiny First Test

Before the full build, let us prove the buzzer works with the smallest possible program. Wire the passive buzzer for a quick test (full wiring comes next): one leg to GPIO 13 (physical pin 33), the other leg to a GND pin. A passive buzzer is gentle enough to drive straight from a GPIO pin — unlike a speaker, which we deal with later.

from gpiozero import TonalBuzzer
from gpiozero.tones import Tone
from time import sleep

buzzer = TonalBuzzer(13)
buzzer.play(Tone("A4"))
sleep(1)
buzzer.stop()

Line 1 — from gpiozero import TonalBuzzer: Imports the object for a passive buzzer.

Line 2 — from gpiozero.tones import Tone: Imports the Tone class so we can name a note.

Line 3 — from time import sleep: sleep(seconds) pauses the program. We use it to hold the note for a moment.

Line 5 — buzzer = TonalBuzzer(13): Creates a buzzer object on GPIO 13 (physical pin 33). The number 13 is the BCM/GPIO number, as always in gpiozero.

Line 6 — buzzer.play(Tone("A4")): Starts playing the note A4. The buzzer keeps sounding until we tell it to stop.

Line 7 — sleep(1): Waits one second while the note sounds.

Line 8 — buzzer.stop(): Silences the buzzer. Always stop a tone when you are done, or it sounds forever.

Save it as beep_test.py in Thonny and press the green Run button. You should hear a clear one-second tone. If you do, your salvaged sounder lives — congratulations. If not, jump ahead to Troubleshooting.

  • A passive buzzer — salvaged from a greeting card, toy, or PC motherboard (see "From the e-waste pile"), or a new one from a starter kit.

Tip: If your kit included a small breadboard-friendly buzzer, it is most likely passive (the musical kind). Test it with the coin-cell trick to be sure.

Wiring the Passive Buzzer

Careful: Power off and unplug your Pi before wiring. Shut down from the desktop menu, wait for the green light to go out, then remove the power lead. The Pi's GPIO is 3.3 V logic — never feed more than 3.3 V into a pin. A small passive buzzer draws little current and is safe to drive directly; but a real loudspeaker or any motor must never be wired straight to a GPIO pin — that comes later, through a transistor. Only re-power once your wiring is checked.

Connection table

FromToWire colourWhy
GPIO 13 (physical pin 33)Buzzer leg 1 (signal)PurpleCarries the PWM switching signal that sets the pitch
Buzzer leg 2GND (physical pin 39)GreyCompletes the circuit back to ground
Note: A passive buzzer has no polarity that matters for sound — you can swap the two legs and it still plays. (An active buzzer does care which leg is +.) GPIO 13 is one of the Pi's hardware-PWM-capable pins, which makes for a clean tone, but gpiozero works on any GPIO pin.

Wiring steps

  1. Shut down and unplug the Pi. Wait for the green activity light to go out before touching any wiring.
  2. Seat the buzzer. Push the buzzer's two legs into two different rows of the breadboard so the legs are not shorted together.
  3. Run the signal wire. Female end of a jumper onto physical pin 33 (GPIO 13); male end into the breadboard row holding buzzer leg 1.
  4. Run the GND wire. Female end of a second jumper onto physical pin 39 (a GND pin); male end into the row holding buzzer leg 2.
  5. Double-check that the two buzzer legs are in separate rows and no metal is touching. Then reconnect power and boot up.

Python Fundamentals: Dictionaries, Functions, Loops

Let us learn the three Python ideas with tiny examples before the big program.

Dictionaries — pairing keys with values

A list stores items in order, fetched by position. A dictionary stores items as key → value pairs, fetched by name. That is perfect for "note name → frequency".

notes = {"C4": 262, "D4": 294, "E4": 330}
print(notes["D4"])   # 294

Line 1 — notes = {…}: The curly braces { } create a dictionary. Each entry is a key (the note name, in quotes) then a colon then its value (the frequency). Commas separate the entries.

Line 2 — notes["D4"]: Look up a value by its key inside square brackets. Asking for "D4" hands back 294. No need to remember any position — you ask by name.

In plain words: A dictionary is like a real dictionary: you look up a word (the key) to get its definition (the value). Keys must be unique. Here the keys are note names and the values are frequencies in hertz.

Functions — name a job once, reuse it

A function wraps up a few lines under a name so you can run them again and again without repeating yourself.

def greet(name):
    print("Hello, " + name)

greet("Ada")
greet("Linus")

Line 1 — def greet(name):: def defines a function called greet. The word in brackets, name, is a parameter — a placeholder for a value you pass in when you call it.

Line 2 — print("Hello, " + name): The indented body. Whatever name holds gets joined onto the greeting and printed.

Lines 4–5 — greet("Ada") / greet("Linus"): Two calls. Each runs the same function body with a different value for name. One definition, many uses.

Loops — repeat without copy-paste

A for loop walks through a collection one item at a time.

for note in ["C4", "E4", "G4"]:
    print("Playing", note)

Line 1 — for note in […]:: Take each item of the list in turn and put it in the variable note. The loop body runs once per item.

Line 2 — print("Playing", note): The indented body runs three times — once with note equal to "C4", then "E4", then "G4".

A list of tuples — storing a melody

A tuple is a small fixed bundle of values written in round brackets, like ("C4", 0.5). We store a melody as a list of these tuples: each one is a note plus how long to hold it.

melody = [("C4", 0.5), ("E4", 0.5), ("G4", 1.0)]
for note, length in melody:
    print(note, "for", length, "seconds")

Line 1 — melody = [ (…), (…) ]: A list (square brackets) whose items are tuples (round brackets). Each tuple pairs a note name with a duration in seconds.

Line 2 — for note, length in melody:: As the loop takes each tuple, it unpacks the two values into note and length in one step. This is why tuples are so handy for paired data.

Line 3 — print(...): Runs once per tuple, with the note and its length already separated out for us.

The Full Program

Now we combine everything: a dictionary of notes, a play_note() function, a loop to play a scale, and a melody stored as a list of tuples. Open a new file in Thonny and type it in.

from gpiozero import TonalBuzzer
from gpiozero.tones import Tone
from time import sleep

# Map each note name to its frequency in hertz
NOTES = {
    "C4": 262, "D4": 294, "E4": 330, "F4": 349,
    "G4": 392, "A4": 440, "B4": 494, "C5": 523,
    "REST": None,
}

buzzer = TonalBuzzer(13)


def play_note(name, length):
    """Play one note for a number of seconds, then fall silent."""
    if name == "REST":
        buzzer.stop()
    else:
        frequency = NOTES[name]
        buzzer.play(Tone(frequency))
    sleep(length)
    buzzer.stop()


def play_scale():
    """Play the eight notes of a C major scale, low to high."""
    for name in ["C4", "D4", "E4", "F4", "G4", "A4", "B4", "C5"]:
        print("Scale:", name)
        play_note(name, 0.4)


def play_melody(melody):
    """Play a melody given as a list of (note, length) tuples."""
    for name, length in melody:
        print("Melody:", name)
        play_note(name, length)


# "Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star" as a list of (note, length) tuples
TWINKLE = [
    ("C4", 0.5), ("C4", 0.5), ("G4", 0.5), ("G4", 0.5),
    ("A4", 0.5), ("A4", 0.5), ("G4", 1.0),
    ("F4", 0.5), ("F4", 0.5), ("E4", 0.5), ("E4", 0.5),
    ("D4", 0.5), ("D4", 0.5), ("C4", 1.0),
]

print("Playing a scale...")
play_scale()
sleep(0.5)

print("Now a tune...")
play_melody(TWINKLE)

buzzer.stop()
print("Done!")

Lines 1–3 — the imports: TonalBuzzer drives the passive buzzer, Tone describes a pitch, and sleep times each note.

Lines 6–10 — NOTES = {…}: Our dictionary mapping note names to frequencies. The special key "REST" maps to None (Python's "nothing"), which we will treat as a silent gap.

Line 12 — buzzer = TonalBuzzer(13): Creates the buzzer object on GPIO 13. We make it once, near the top, so every function can use it.

Line 15 — def play_note(name, length):: Defines our key function. It takes two parameters: the note name and how long (length) to hold it.

Line 16 — the docstring: The triple-quoted text just under def describes what the function does. It is documentation, not code.

Lines 17–18 — if name == "REST": buzzer.stop(): If the note is a rest, keep the buzzer silent.

Lines 19–21 — the else branch: Otherwise, look up the frequency for that name in the NOTES dictionary and start playing it with buzzer.play(Tone(frequency)).

Line 22 — sleep(length): Hold the note (or the silence) for the requested number of seconds.

Line 23 — buzzer.stop(): Silence the buzzer before the next note, giving each note a clean, separate sound.

Lines 26–30 — def play_scale():: A function with no parameters. It loops over a list of eight note names and calls play_note() for each at 0.4 seconds. This is loops + functions + dictionary working together.

Lines 33–37 — def play_melody(melody):: Takes a melody (a list of tuples). The for name, length in melody: line unpacks each tuple into its two parts, then plays it. The same function can play any melody you pass it.

Lines 41–46 — TWINKLE = [...]: The tune stored as data — a list of (note, length) tuples. Storing music as data means the playing code never changes; only the data does.

Lines 48–55 — the main part: Print a message, play the scale, pause half a second, then play the tune by handing TWINKLE to play_melody().

Lines 57–58 — buzzer.stop() / print("Done!"): A final tidy-up to make sure the buzzer is silent, and a friendly message.

Why a Speaker Needs a Transistor

A passive buzzer is happy on a GPIO pin because it sips only a milliamp or two. A proper loudspeaker — the kind in a radio or headphones — is a different beast. To be loud it needs much more current than a GPIO pin can safely give.

Careful: A GPIO pin can supply only about 16 mA, and the whole header together only around 50 mA. A small 8 Ω speaker can try to pull hundreds of milliamps. Wiring a speaker (or any motor) straight to a GPIO pin will overload the pin and can permanently damage your Pi. Speakers and motors are also inductive: when the current switches off they kick back a brief high-voltage spike that can punch through a GPIO pin. Always drive them through a transistor.
In plain words: A transistor is an electronic switch with no moving parts. A tiny current from the GPIO pin (into the transistor's base) controls a much bigger current flowing from the 5 V supply through the speaker. The Pi sends a small, safe signal; the transistor does the heavy lifting and the Pi is protected.

We use a common, cheap NPN transistor — a 2N2222 or BC548. It has three legs: base, collector, and emitter. The GPIO pin feeds the base through a 1 kΩ resistor (which limits the small base current and protects the pin). The speaker connects between the 5 V supply and the transistor's collector; the emitter goes to GND. When the GPIO pin pulses, the transistor switches the speaker current on and off in time — and the speaker plays the note.

Note: The 2N2222 and BC548 have their three legs in different orders, so always check the part's datasheet pinout (looking at the flat face, legs are typically Emitter–Base–Collector or Collector–Base–Emitter). Wiring the legs in the wrong order is the most common reason a transistor circuit stays silent.

The lovely part: the code does not change at all. Your Pi still runs the exact same sound_machine.py with TonalBuzzer(13). From the pin's point of view it is still just switching a single GPIO line on and off — the transistor and 5 V supply quietly turn that into a loud speaker note. That is the whole point of a transistor driver: it lets a tiny, safe signal control a big load.

Make It Your Own

You have a working instrument. Here are four ways to take it further.

  • Add more notes. Extend the NOTES dictionary with sharps and flats (e.g. "F#4": 370) and a lower octave, then write a tune that uses them.
  • Store songs in a file. Keep each melody as its own list of tuples and add a menu: print the song names, ask the user to type a number, then play the chosen one.
  • Two-part rhythm with an active buzzer. If you also salvaged an active buzzer, wire it to another pin as a Buzzer and beep() it on the beat for a simple drum.
  • Build a one-key keyboard. Add a push button (from Chapter 12) so each press plays the next note of a scale — a tiny playable instrument from pure e-waste.
Tip: Because the melody is stored as data (a list of tuples) and the playing is done by a function, you can add a whole new song without touching the playback code at all. That separation of data from behaviour is one of the most powerful ideas in all of programming — you have just used it for real.

You started with something destined for the bin and ended with a programmable instrument that plays whatever you teach it. The official gpiozero documentation at gpiozero.readthedocs.io lists every sound object if you want to dig deeper. Badge earned: Now It Sings.

Key terms

  • Frequency - How many times per second something vibrates, measured in hertz (Hz); it determines the pitch of a note.
  • PWM (Pulse-Width Modulation) - Switching a GPIO pin on and off very fast; the number of cycles per second sets the tone's frequency.
  • Active buzzer - A buzzer with a built-in oscillator that plays one fixed beep; controlled with gpiozero's Buzzer object.
  • Passive buzzer - A buzzer with no oscillator that plays whatever pitch you drive it at; controlled with gpiozero's TonalBuzzer object.
  • TonalBuzzer - The gpiozero object that plays musical notes on a passive buzzer using play() and stop().
  • Tone - gpiozero's way of describing a pitch, by note name ("A4"), frequency (440.0), or MIDI number (69).
  • Dictionary - A Python collection of key→value pairs, looked up by key; here it maps note names to frequencies.
  • Function - A named, reusable block of code that can take parameters and be called many times.
  • Tuple - A small fixed bundle of values in round brackets, e.g. ("C4", 0.5); ideal for paired data like note-and-length.
  • NPN transistor - An electronic switch (e.g. 2N2222, BC548) that lets a small GPIO signal control a much larger current for a speaker or motor.
  • Flyback diode - A diode placed across an inductive load (speaker/motor) to absorb the voltage spike produced when its current switches off.

Chapter summary

  • Sound is air vibrating; the frequency of vibration (in hertz) sets the pitch — 440 Hz is the note A4.
  • A GPIO pin makes a pitch using PWM: switching on and off at the note's frequency.
  • An active buzzer makes one fixed beep (use Buzzer); a passive buzzer can play any note (use TonalBuzzer with Tone).
  • A passive buzzer is gentle enough to wire straight to a GPIO pin (one leg to the pin, one to GND).
  • A Python dictionary neatly maps note names to frequencies; functions let us name and reuse the "play a note" job; loops step through a scale or a melody.
  • A melody is stored as a list of tuples — each tuple a (note, length) pair — keeping the music data separate from the playback code.
  • A real loudspeaker draws far too much current for a GPIO pin and must be driven through an NPN transistor (2N2222/BC548) with a ~1 kΩ base resistor; a flyback diode protects against inductive spikes.
  • Never wire a speaker or motor directly to a GPIO pin — it can permanently damage the Pi.
  • Sounders are easy, safe e-waste to salvage from greeting cards, toys, and old PCs — but never open mains, microwave, or CRT equipment.
