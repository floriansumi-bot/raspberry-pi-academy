In Lesson 44 you built the Junkbox AI Rover and drove it from a web remote in your hand. In this chapter you give it ears. You will speak the words "forward", "left", "right" or "stop" out loud, a USB microphone will catch them, and a tiny offline speech engine called Vosk will turn your voice into text — entirely on the Pi, with no internet and nothing sent to a cloud. Along the way you will learn the Python that ties a live microphone to a robot: a microphone capture loop with sounddevice, a recogniser that streams audio to text, keyword matching to find a command inside a sentence, and a dispatch dictionary that maps each command word to a method on the Robot class you already wrote.

What You'll Build & Learn

By the end of this lesson your rover obeys spoken commands. Say "forward" and it rolls; say "stop" and it halts; say "left" or "right" and it spins. Everything runs offline on the Pi 5 itself, fast enough to feel live. You will not rewire a single motor — the driving is done in Lesson 44. What is new is the voice front-end: the software glue from microphone to motor.

The whole pipeline is four stages: a microphone feeds raw sound into Vosk, which turns it into text; you match keywords in that text; and a matched keyword calls a method on your imported Robot. One small Python program holds it all.

Python fundamentals (named)

  • A microphone capture loop — using the sounddevice library to open a live audio stream at 16 kHz mono and feed it, block by block, into a recogniser. This is the same shape as Lesson 44's camera loop, but for sound instead of pictures.
  • Streaming speech-to-text with Vosk — a KaldiRecognizer object that accepts audio and hands back recognised words as JSON, with partial results as you speak and a final result when you pause.
  • Keyword matching — pulling a known command out of a recognised sentence by splitting it into words and checking each against a set. This is plain Python string handling, no AI required.
  • A dispatch dictionary — a dictionary that maps each command word (the key) to the function it should run (the value). Instead of a long ladder of if/elif, you look the word up and call what you find. It reinforces functions and the classes from Lesson 44.

AI / audio fundamentals (named)

  • Offline speech-to-text — why running the recogniser on the device (rather than sending your voice to a cloud service) matters for privacy and for working with no internet, and why Vosk with its small model is the right live-command tool on a Pi 5, where the heavier Whisper from Lesson 19 is not.
  • Constraining the vocabulary — telling Vosk to listen for only a fixed list of words. A recogniser that can only hear five commands is both faster and far more accurate than one trying to transcribe every word in English.

Hardware fundamentals (named)

  • Audio input on the Pi 5 — the honest truth that the Pi 5 has no analogue audio input and no on-board microphone, so you must add a USB microphone, and how to confirm the Pi can see it.

Why Offline Speech Recognition?

When you talk to a smart speaker, your voice is usually recorded, sent over the internet to a company's servers, turned into text there, and the answer sent back. That has three costs: it needs a live internet connection, it adds delay while the audio travels there and back, and your voice leaves your home and lands on someone else's computer.

A robot that obeys voice commands should not depend on any of that. It might roam a garden with no Wi-Fi; you might not want a microphone streaming audio to a company; and a command like "stop" needs to act now, not after a round trip to a data centre. So we do the recognition on the Pi itself — called offline (or on-device) speech-to-text.

In plain words: Speech-to-text (STT), also called speech recognition, is software that listens to sound and writes down the words it hears. Offline means it does the whole job on your own device, with nothing sent over the internet — so it works with the Wi-Fi switched off, and your voice never leaves the Pi.

Vosk vs Whisper: which tool for live commands?

You already met one offline speech-to-text tool in Lesson 19: Whisper (via the fast whisper.cpp build). Whisper is wonderfully accurate, but it is built to transcribe a recording — you hand it a whole audio file, it thinks for a while, and gives back the text. That batch style is perfect for voice memos, but it is the wrong shape for a robot: you do not want to record yourself, wait, then have the rover move.

For live commands we want a streaming recogniser — one that listens continuously and reports words the instant you say them. That is Vosk (from the alphacephei project). Vosk is designed for real-time recognition, ships ready-built for the Pi's 64-bit processor, and offers a tiny small English model of about 40–50 MB that runs comfortably on a Pi 5. (Vosk also has a large model of around 1.8 GB; that one is far too heavy for live use on a Pi, so we never use it here.)

The Pi 5 Has No Microphone

Here is a piece of hardware honesty that trips up many beginners. The Raspberry Pi 5 has no analogue audio input and no built-in microphone. There is no 3.5 mm microphone jack (the Pi 5 dropped even the old combined audio jack), and nothing on the board can hear you. Software alone cannot conjure a microphone the Pi does not have.

The fix is simple and cheap: a USB microphone. On Raspberry Pi OS Bookworm a USB mic is plug-and-play — push it into any USB port and the operating system recognises it as a sound input device. A small USB "mini-mic", a USB headset, or a USB webcam with a built-in microphone all work, and USB is hot-pluggable so you can connect it at any time.

Careful — you cannot skip the USB microphone: The Pi 5 has no microphone of its own and no analogue audio input, so the voice code in this lesson will not work until you plug a USB microphone into the Pi. There is no 3.5 mm mic socket to use instead. If you try to run the recogniser with no USB mic attached, sounddevice will report that there is no input device. This is a hardware fact, not a bug in your code.

Check the Pi can hear the microphone

Before writing any Python, confirm at the terminal that the operating system sees your microphone. The command arecord -l lists every capture (recording) device. Open a terminal (Raspberry Pi menu → Accessories → Terminal) and run:

arecord -l

arecord -l: arecord is the built-in audio recorder on Raspberry Pi OS; the -l flag means "list the capture devices". If your USB mic is connected you will see a line like card 3: Device [USB PnP Sound Device], device 0: .... If instead it prints no soundcards found, the Pi cannot see any microphone — re-seat the USB plug or try another port, then run the command again.

Once a card appears, do a five-second test recording to be completely sure the microphone is working. Speak while it records:

arecord --format=S16_LE --duration=5 --rate=16000 --file-type=wav mic_test.wav
aplay mic_test.wav

Line 1 — arecord --format=S16_LE --duration=5 --rate=16000 --file-type=wav mic_test.wav: Records five seconds of sound to a file called mic_test.wav. --format=S16_LE means 16-bit samples (the format Vosk expects), --rate=16000 means 16,000 samples per second — the 16 kHz rate speech recognisers are built for — and --file-type=wav saves a standard WAV file. Speak normally while it records.

Line 2 — aplay mic_test.wav: Plays the recording back through the Pi's audio output so you can hear yourself. If you hear your voice clearly, the microphone is working and at the exact sample rate Vosk wants. If it is silent or very faint, move the mic closer or pick a different USB port, and check the input level in the Raspberry Pi audio settings.

In plain words: 16 kHz mono describes the audio we feed the recogniser. 16 kHz (16,000 samples per second) is the sampling rate speech models are trained on — enough to capture human speech, no more. Mono means a single channel (one microphone), as opposed to stereo's two. Matching these exactly is important: feed Vosk the wrong rate and the words come out as gibberish.

Installing Vosk in a Virtual Environment

We need two Python libraries that are not on the Pi by default: vosk (the speech engine) and sounddevice (which reads the microphone). As you saw in Lesson 44, Bookworm follows a rule called PEP 668 that blocks a plain pip install into the system Python, to stop you breaking the operating system's own packages. The answer is the same habit as before: install into a virtual environment (a venv).

In plain words: a virtual environment is a private, throwaway copy of Python in its own folder. Packages you pip install while it is active land inside that folder, not in the system Python — so you can add libraries freely without any risk to Raspberry Pi OS.

There is one wrinkle that matters for a robot. Your rover's drive code uses gpiozero, which lives in the system Python, not inside a fresh venv. A plain venv cannot see it, so from gpiozero import Motor would fail. The fix — exactly as in Lesson 44 — is to create the venv with the --system-site-packages flag, which lets the venv also see the system's packages (including gpiozero) while still letting you pip-install your own on top.

  1. Create the venv with access to the system packages. In a terminal, run python3 -m venv --system-site-packages ~/voice. The --system-site-packages flag is the important part: without it, the venv is sealed off and your robot's gpiozero import will fail.
  2. Activate it. Run source ~/voice/bin/activate. Your prompt gains a (voice) prefix, showing that pip now installs into this environment.
  3. Install the two libraries. Run pip install vosk sounddevice. Vosk ships ready-built packages for the Pi's 64-bit processor, so this downloads in moments with nothing to compile.
  4. Check gpiozero is still visible. Run python3 -c "import gpiozero, vosk, sounddevice; print('all ok')". If it prints all ok, the venv can see both your freshly installed libraries and the system's gpiozero — exactly what the robot needs.
Careful: If you create the venv without --system-site-packages, everything in this lesson runs right up until the robot part, then from gpiozero import Motor dies with ModuleNotFoundError: No module named 'gpiozero'. If that happens, you do not need to start over — either recreate the venv with the flag, or run pip install gpiozero lgpio inside the existing venv to install gpiozero there too. Either fix works.

The Vosk code and the Vosk model are separate downloads on purpose, so you can pick a model to suit your device. On a Pi 5, the small English model is the sweet spot: light enough to run live, accurate enough for clear commands.

The Mic-to-Motors Pipeline

Before any code, picture the whole journey of a spoken command. Four stages, left to right.

Stage 2 (Vosk) and stage 4 (the motors) are libraries someone else wrote — you simply call them. Your job is the small, readable glue: stage 1's capture loop and stage 3's keyword match. We will build each piece on its own, then join them.

Capturing the Microphone with sounddevice

The first piece is the microphone capture loop: open the USB mic as a live stream of audio and feed it, a block at a time, into Vosk. The sounddevice library does the capturing; Vosk does the recognising.

Audio arrives in blocks — small chunks of samples. sounddevice hands each block to a little function (a callback) the instant it is ready, and the cleanest way to pass those blocks to the main loop is a queue: the callback drops each block on, the loop takes them off one by one. This is the standard, official Vosk pattern.

In plain words: a callback is a function you hand to a library and say "call this for me whenever something happens" — here, whenever a new block of audio is ready. A queue is a tidy waiting line: the callback adds blocks at one end, the main loop takes them from the other, so the two never trip over each other.

Let us first prove the recogniser works on its own. Save this as listen.py in your project folder (the same folder that holds the vosk-model-small-en-us-0.22 folder). It listens to the mic and prints every word it hears — no robot yet.

# listen.py  --  print whatever the microphone hears, fully offline
import queue
import json
import sounddevice as sd
from vosk import Model, KaldiRecognizer

SAMPLE_RATE = 16000
MODEL_PATH = "vosk-model-small-en-us-0.22"

# A thread-safe queue the audio callback fills and the main loop drains.
audio_q = queue.Queue()


def callback(indata, frames, time, status):
    """Called by sounddevice for every block of audio from the mic."""
    if status:
        print(status)                 # report dropped audio, but keep going
    audio_q.put(bytes(indata))        # hand the raw block to the main loop


model = Model(MODEL_PATH)
recogniser = KaldiRecognizer(model, SAMPLE_RATE)

print("Listening - say something. Press Ctrl+C to stop.")
with sd.RawInputStream(samplerate=SAMPLE_RATE, blocksize=8000,
                       dtype="int16", channels=1, callback=callback):
    while True:
        data = audio_q.get()
        if recogniser.AcceptWaveform(data):
            result = json.loads(recogniser.Result())
            text = result.get("text", "")
            if text:
                print("Heard:", text)
        else:
            partial = json.loads(recogniser.PartialResult())
            if partial.get("partial"):
                print("  ...", partial["partial"], end="\r")

Lines 2–5 — imports: queue for the waiting line between the callback and the loop; json because Vosk returns its results as JSON text; sounddevice (shortened to sd) to read the mic; and from Vosk the Model (the language data) and KaldiRecognizer (the engine that turns audio into text).

Line 7 — SAMPLE_RATE = 16000: The 16 kHz rate the small model expects. The microphone, the recogniser and this number must all agree; a mismatch produces nonsense.

Line 8 — MODEL_PATH = "...": The folder you unzipped earlier. Because it is a plain folder name, Vosk looks for it beside this program.

Line 11 — audio_q = queue.Queue(): Creates the queue. It is thread-safe, meaning the callback and the main loop can use it at the same time without clashing.

Lines 14–18 — def callback(...): The function sounddevice runs for every block of audio. indata is the raw block; if status is set, some audio was dropped, so we print a warning but carry on. audio_q.put(bytes(indata)) drops the block onto the queue for the main loop to collect.

Line 21 — model = Model(MODEL_PATH): Loads the small English model from that folder into memory. This takes a second or two; do it once, before the loop.

Line 22 — recogniser = KaldiRecognizer(model, SAMPLE_RATE): Builds the recogniser, telling it both which model to use and the sample rate to expect. This object does the actual audio-to-text work.

Lines 25–26 — with sd.RawInputStream(...): Opens the live microphone stream. samplerate=16000 matches the model; blocksize=8000 is half a second per block; dtype="int16" is the 16-bit format Vosk wants; channels=1 is mono; callback=callback wires in our function. The with block keeps the stream open while we loop and closes it cleanly when we stop.

Line 28 — data = audio_q.get(): Take the next block of audio off the queue. If none is ready yet, get() waits, so the loop never spins uselessly.

Lines 29–34 — if recogniser.AcceptWaveform(data): Feed the block to Vosk. AcceptWaveform returns True when the recogniser decides a phrase is finished (you paused). Then Result() gives the final text as JSON; we parse it with json.loads and read the "text" field. If there is text, we print it.

Lines 35–38 — the partial: While you are still speaking, AcceptWaveform returns False; PartialResult() gives the best guess so far. Printing it with end="\r" overwrites the same line, so you watch the words form as you talk. The partial is a nice live preview; the final result is what we act on.

Activate your venv if it is not already (source ~/voice/bin/activate), then run python3 listen.py and speak. You should see your words appear. This little program is the whole front-end — everything else is deciding what to do with the text.

Tip: If sounddevice grabs the wrong input (you have a webcam mic and a USB mic, say), list the devices with python3 -c "import sounddevice as sd; print(sd.query_devices())". Each line has a number; pass the one you want as device=N to RawInputStream(...). By default it uses the system's chosen input, which is usually right.

Matching Words to Commands

Vosk hands us a sentence, but a robot wants a single command. If you say "robot go forward please", the recognised text is the whole phrase — we need to fish the word "forward" out of it. That is keyword matching, and it is ordinary Python string handling, no AI involved.

The plan: split the recognised text into individual words, then check each word against the set of commands we understand. The first command word we find wins.

We can teach the keyword-matching idea with no robot at all. Here is a tiny function and a quick test of it:

# a small isolated example of keyword matching
COMMANDS = {"forward", "back", "left", "right", "stop"}


def find_command(text):
    """Return the first known command word in text, or None."""
    for word in text.split():
        if word in COMMANDS:
            return word
    return None


# try it on some sentences Vosk might produce
for phrase in ["go forward please", "now turn left", "lovely weather", "stop"]:
    print(repr(phrase), "->", find_command(phrase))

Line 2 — COMMANDS = {...}: A set of the words we understand. A set is the right tool because checking "is this word in the set?" is instant, however many words it holds, and it cannot hold accidental duplicates.

Line 5 — def find_command(text):: A function that takes a recognised sentence and returns the command hidden in it.

Line 7 — for word in text.split():: split() chops the sentence into a list of words on the spaces. We walk through them one at a time.

Lines 8–9 — if word in COMMANDS: return word: The moment we hit a word that is one of our commands, we return it — so "go forward please" yields "forward". The leading "go" and trailing "please" are simply ignored.

Line 10 — return None: If the loop finishes having found no command, return None to mean "nothing I recognise". The caller checks for this before moving the robot.

Lines 14–15 — the test: Run a few sample phrases through the function and print each result. "lovely weather" returns None; the others return their command. Testing a function in isolation like this, before wiring it to motors, is how you build confidence one piece at a time.

Tip: Vosk gives back lower-case text with no punctuation, which is exactly what makes this simple in test reliable. If you later add commands that are two words ("turn around"), match on the whole phrase with text.find("turn around") instead of word-by-word, or add the phrase to a richer dictionary.

Constraining Vosk to Our Words

One elegant trick makes the whole system faster and far more accurate. Instead of letting Vosk try to recognise any English word, we tell it: "you will only ever hear these few words." A recogniser choosing between five words almost never mishears; one choosing between thousands sometimes does.

Vosk supports this through a grammar — a list of the phrases it is allowed to return, passed as a JSON string when you build the recogniser. We give it our command words plus a special "[unk]" token, Vosk's way of saying "anything I do not recognise" — so words outside the list come back as unknown rather than being forced into a wrong command.

In plain words: a grammar here is just a fixed word-list you hand the recogniser so it only listens for those words. The "[unk]" entry is a catch-all for "something else" — including it stops a stray noise being misheard as a real command.

It is a single change to how we build the recogniser. Compare the open version from listen.py with the constrained version we will use for the robot:

# open: recognises any English word (what listen.py used)
recogniser = KaldiRecognizer(model, SAMPLE_RATE)

# constrained: recognises ONLY our command words (plus "[unk]")
grammar = '["forward back left right stop", "[unk]"]'
recogniser = KaldiRecognizer(model, SAMPLE_RATE, grammar)

Line 2 — the open form: With just the model and sample rate, Vosk tries to recognise the whole language. Fine for dictation; needless work for five commands.

Line 5 — grammar = '["forward back left right stop", "[unk]"]': A JSON string holding a list. The first entry lists the words Vosk may hear (space-separated inside one string); the second, "[unk]", is the unknown-word catch-all. The single quotes outside make it a Python string; the double quotes inside are the JSON.

Line 6 — KaldiRecognizer(model, SAMPLE_RATE, grammar): Passing the grammar as the third argument switches the recogniser into "only these words" mode. From now on it returns one of our commands, or "[unk]" for anything else — quicker and more reliable than the open recogniser.

Note: This word-list trick works because we are using the small model, which supports a custom vocabulary at start-up. It is the single biggest reason Vosk feels so snappy for command work: you have turned a general-purpose dictation engine into a purpose-built five-word listener.

Putting It Together: The Voice Rover

Now we join the three pieces — the capture loop, the constrained recogniser, and keyword matching — and point the result at the Robot class from Lesson 44. The link between a command word and a robot action is a dispatch dictionary: the word is the key, and the function to run is the value.

In plain words: a dispatch dictionary maps choices to actions. Instead of a long if word == "forward": ... elif word == "left": ... ladder, you keep a dictionary {"forward": go_forward, "left": go_left, ...} and just look the word up to find the function to call. Adding a new command becomes one new line, not another elif.

Make sure robot.py from Lesson 44 is in the same folder — we import its Robot class unchanged. Its methods are forward(), backward(), turn_left(), turn_right() and stop(), exactly the actions our commands need. Save the program below as voice_rover.py.

Careful — wheels off the ground for the first test. As in Lesson 44, prop the chassis on a mug or box so the wheels spin freely in the air before you give any spoken command. A mis-heard word should never be able to drive the rover off a table. Only put it on the floor once the voice commands behave as you expect.
# voice_rover.py  --  drive the Lesson 44 rover by voice, fully offline
import queue
import json
import sounddevice as sd
from vosk import Model, KaldiRecognizer
from robot import Robot          # the drive base from Lesson 44

SAMPLE_RATE = 16000
MODEL_PATH = "vosk-model-small-en-us-0.22"

# Build the rover on the same pins as Lesson 44 (wheels off the ground!).
robot = Robot(left_pins=(20, 21), right_pins=(19, 26))

# The dispatch dictionary: each command word maps to a robot action.
ACTIONS = {
    "forward": robot.forward,
    "back": robot.backward,
    "left": robot.turn_left,
    "right": robot.turn_right,
    "stop": robot.stop,
}

audio_q = queue.Queue()


def callback(indata, frames, time, status):
    if status:
        print(status)
    audio_q.put(bytes(indata))


def find_command(text):
    """Return the first word in text that is one of our commands."""
    for word in text.split():
        if word in ACTIONS:
            return word
    return None


# Constrain Vosk to only our command words, plus the unknown catch-all.
grammar = '["forward back left right stop", "[unk]"]'
model = Model(MODEL_PATH)
recogniser = KaldiRecognizer(model, SAMPLE_RATE, grammar)

print("Voice rover ready. Say: forward / back / left / right / stop.")
print("Press Ctrl+C to quit.")
try:
    with sd.RawInputStream(samplerate=SAMPLE_RATE, blocksize=8000,
                           dtype="int16", channels=1, callback=callback):
        while True:
            data = audio_q.get()
            if recogniser.AcceptWaveform(data):
                text = json.loads(recogniser.Result()).get("text", "")
                command = find_command(text)
                if command:
                    print("Command:", command)
                    ACTIONS[command]()      # look up and run the action
except KeyboardInterrupt:
    robot.stop()
    print("\nStopped.")

Lines 2–6 — imports: the same audio and Vosk imports as before, plus from robot import Robot — the Lesson 44 module, imported unchanged. The whole rover front-end is assembled from imports.

Line 12 — robot = Robot(left_pins=(20, 21), right_pins=(19, 26)): Builds the drive base on the exact pins from Lesson 44's recap table. Naming the arguments keeps the two sides from getting muddled.

Lines 15–21 — ACTIONS = {...}: The dispatch dictionary. Each key is a command word; each value is the method itself — note robot.forward with no brackets, so we store the function to call later rather than calling it now.

Lines 26–29 — callback: Identical to listen.py — every audio block is dropped onto the queue for the main loop.

Lines 32–37 — find_command: The keyword matcher from earlier, now checking against the ACTIONS keys directly — so the set of commands and the set of actions can never drift apart.

Line 41 — grammar = '...': The fixed word-list. Vosk will only return our five commands or "[unk]", which is what makes recognition quick and reliable.

Lines 42–43 — build the recogniser: Load the model and create the KaldiRecognizer with the grammar as its third argument — the constrained mode.

Lines 48–49 — open the stream: the same 16 kHz mono RawInputStream as before, wrapped in try so Ctrl+C can stop us cleanly.

Line 51 — data = audio_q.get(): Take the next audio block off the queue, waiting if none is ready.

Lines 52–57 — recognise and act: When AcceptWaveform says a phrase is finished, read its "text", pull out the command with find_command, and if there is one, ACTIONS[command]() — look the word up in the dictionary and call the function it points to. That one line replaces a whole ladder of if/elif.

Lines 58–60 — clean stop: Ctrl+C stops the motors so the rover never keeps rolling after the program ends. Always end a robot program with a stop.

With the wheels propped up, activate the venv and run python3 voice_rover.py. Say "forward" — the wheels spin forward; say "stop" — they halt. You are talking to your robot, and not one byte of your voice has left the Pi.

Note — commands latch, they do not time out. As written, "forward" keeps the wheels rolling until you say another command (such as "stop"). That is deliberate and simple. If you would rather each command run for a fixed burst — "forward" meaning "go forward for one second" — add a sleep and a robot.stop() after the action. We leave that as one of the "make it your own" ideas below.

Optional: A Wake Word Gate

One snag with an always-listening robot is that ordinary conversation can trip it: say "let's go forward to the shop" and the rover rolls. Smart speakers solve this with a wake word — they ignore everything until they hear a trigger like "hey robot", and only then listen for a command. You can add the same gate, still fully offline, using Picovoice's Porcupine engine.

In plain words: a wake word is a special phrase that "wakes" the robot up — only after it hears the wake word does it pay attention to the next command. It stops the rover reacting to everyday chatter.

Porcupine is an on-device wake-word detector that runs on the Raspberry Pi. You install it with pip install pvporcupine inside the same venv, sign up for a free AccessKey from the Picovoice Console, and can even design a custom phrase such as "hey robot" there. The shape of the program becomes: Porcupine listens for the wake word; once it fires, you run the Vosk command loop above for a few seconds to catch the command, then go back to waiting. We leave the full code as an optional extra — a fine next project once the voice rover works.

Note: Porcupine needs that free AccessKey to run, so unlike Vosk it touches Picovoice's servers once to validate your key. The wake-word detection itself runs on the Pi. If keeping everything strictly offline matters to you, the always-listening Vosk loop with the constrained grammar is already a perfectly good robot — the wake word is a convenience, not a requirement.

Troubleshooting

No microphone detected

  • Run arecord -l first. If it says no soundcards found, the Pi cannot see any mic. Re-seat the USB plug, try a different USB port, and run the command again. Remember the Pi 5 has no built-in mic — without a USB one, there is simply nothing to detect.
  • Python error "no input device". If sounddevice says there is no default input, no microphone is selected. Plug in the USB mic, check it appears in the taskbar audio menu, or list devices with python3 -c "import sounddevice as sd; print(sd.query_devices())" and pass the right one as device=N.
  • It records but plays back silent. The input level may be at zero. Open the audio settings (right-click the taskbar volume icon) and raise the capture level, then redo the arecord test.

Poor accuracy — commands missed or mistaken

  • Use the constrained grammar. If you are still on the open recogniser from listen.py, switch to the grammar version — restricting Vosk to the five words is the single biggest accuracy win.
  • Keep the mic close and the room quiet. A small USB mic across the room with a fan running will struggle. Speak from 20–30 cm away, clearly, and reduce background noise.
  • Check the sample rate matches. The mic, the RawInputStream and the KaldiRecognizer must all be 16,000 Hz. A mismatch makes recognition fall apart — you will see odd or empty text.
  • Pick clearer command words. Words that sound alike get confused. "Stop" and "forward" are distinct; if "back" is unreliable, use "reverse". Update both the grammar and the ACTIONS dictionary together.
  • Confirm you downloaded the small model. The path in MODEL_PATH must point at the unzipped vosk-model-small-en-us-0.22 folder. A wrong path raises an error when Model(...) loads.
Careful: If a mis-heard command ever makes the rover lurch unexpectedly, say "stop" — and if in doubt, pull the motor battery. Keep the wheels off the ground until you trust the recognition in your room, with your microphone, with your voice. Voice control is forgiving when the wheels are in the air and unforgiving when they are on a table edge.

Make It Your Own

The voice rover is a foundation. Here are four ways to build on it, from easy to ambitious.

  • Timed bursts instead of latching. Make "forward" mean "drive forward for one second, then stop". After ACTIONS[command](), add from time import sleep at the top and, for the movement commands, sleep(1) then robot.stop(). The rover now nudges rather than runs, which is safer indoors.
  • More commands. Add words like "spin" (a full turn), "slow" and "fast" (change robot.speed), or "left a bit" (a short turn). For each new word: add it to the grammar string, add it to the ACTIONS dictionary with a small function, and you are done — the dispatch design means no if-ladder to edit.
  • A wake word. Add the Porcupine gate from the optional section so the rover only listens after "hey robot". This stops everyday conversation triggering it — the polish that turns a demo into something you would actually live with.
  • Voice on top of the state machine. Recall Lesson 44's three modes (manual, follow, avoid). Add the words "follow" and "wander" to flip STATE["mode"] from your voice, then "stop" to grab back control. Voice becomes just another input to the same brain.
Where this fits. You have given the Junkbox rover a fourth way to be controlled — alongside the web remote, the colour follower and the obstacle-dodger of Lesson 44. The deeper lesson is the pattern: a hard capability (speech recognition) hidden behind a small, readable front-end. That four-stage shape — sense, interpret, decide, act — is the skeleton of almost every interactive machine you will build.
