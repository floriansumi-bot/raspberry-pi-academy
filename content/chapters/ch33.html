Welcome to the Maker Lab — the part of the course where you stop reading about electronics and start building real things you can hold, switch on, and show off. Over the lessons that follow you will build a traffic light, a reaction game, a singing sound machine, a glowing mood lamp, a parking sensor, a plant logger, a motion alarm, and finally a web dashboard you can open on your phone. Every build teaches a named Python idea and a named electronics idea, and a thread running through all of them is one of the most satisfying skills in the maker world: bringing dead electronics back to life by salvaging their parts. This first lesson is your kit-out and safety briefing — no complex wiring yet, just the toolkit, the languages on your Pi, and the rules that will keep you (and your Pi) safe.

How the Maker Lab Works

Every chapter in this Part is a complete, working project. You will not just copy code — each build introduces a fundamental and teaches it properly with a tiny example first, then puts it to work in the project. By the end you will have written real programs that:

  • switch lights on and off in sequences (loops and lists);
  • read buttons and time your reactions (conditionals, randomness, timing);
  • play tunes through a salvaged speaker (dictionaries, tone generation);
  • fade colours on a recycled-jar lamp (functions, PWM brightness);
  • measure distance and log data to a file (classes, files, timestamps);
  • detect motion and raise an alarm (event-driven callbacks);
  • and serve all of it as a live web page from your Pi (a little HTML, CSS and JavaScript).
In plain words: a fundamental is a core idea you will reuse forever. A for loop, a function, a resistor, LED polarity — learn each one once, properly, and it pays off in every project after. We will always name the fundamental out loud so you know exactly what you are learning.

You do not need a fortune in parts. A modest beginner starter kit — a breadboard, a handful of jumper wires, some LEDs, resistors, a couple of buttons, and a cheap multimeter — covers most of the Part. The rest you can salvage from broken electronics destined for the bin, which is exactly what the recycling thread is about. By the end of this lesson you will know how to tell a treasure-filled gadget from a genuinely dangerous one.

The Maker Toolkit

Let us meet the tools and parts you will use again and again. None of them is expensive, and most come in a starter kit. The figure below labels the essentials.

The breadboard

In plain words: a breadboard is a plastic board full of holes with hidden metal strips inside. You push wires and component legs into the holes and the metal connects them — no soldering, no glue, and you can pull everything out and start again. It is the workbench for every build in this Part.

Inside, the holes are connected in groups. The two long rails down each edge (marked + and −) are the power rails — every hole along a rail is joined. In the central area, each short numbered half-row is joined across (e.g. holes 10a–10e), but a gap down the middle separates the left half from the right half. You will use this layout in every wiring project from the next chapter onward; for now, just know that pushing two legs into the same half-row connects them.

Jumper wires

Jumper wires are short flexible wires with rigid connectors on each end. They come in three flavours, named by what is on each end:

TypeEndsTypical use
Male-to-male (M–M)a pin on both endsbreadboard hole → breadboard hole
Female-to-male (F–M)a socket on one end, a pin on the otherPi GPIO pin (needs the socket) → breadboard hole
Female-to-female (F–F)a socket on both endsmodule header pin → Pi GPIO pin directly
Tip: the Pi's GPIO header is a row of pins, so any wire going to the Pi needs a female (socket) end there. For connecting the Pi to a breadboard, female-to-male wires are what you want. A small bundle of each type will see you through the whole Part.

Resistors, LEDs and buttons

A resistor slows electricity down. Its job in nearly every project here is to protect an LED (and the GPIO pin) by limiting current. The standard beginner value is 330 Ω for an LED on a 3.3 V pin; you will also meet 1 kΩ as a base resistor for transistors later.

In plain words: an LED (Light-Emitting Diode) is a tiny lamp that only lets current flow one way. It has a longer leg (the anode, +) and a shorter leg with a flat edge on the plastic rim (the cathode, −). Current must enter the long leg and leave the short leg, or it will not light.

A push button (a tactile switch) is just a momentary connection: press it and two sides join; release and they part. You will wire your first one in the reaction-timer game two chapters from now.

Reading Resistor Colour Bands

Resistors are tiny, so their value is printed as coloured bands rather than numbers. A standard 4-band resistor reads: digit, digit, multiplier, tolerance. The first two bands are digits, the third tells you how many zeros (the multiplier), and the last (set slightly apart, usually gold) is the manufacturing tolerance.

ColourDigitMultiplier
Black0×1
Brown1×10
Red2×100
Orange3×1,000
Yellow4×10,000
Green5×100,000
Blue6×1,000,000
Violet7—
Grey8—
White9—
Gold—tolerance ±5%

Let us read the two values you will use most. A 330 Ω resistor is orange–orange–brown–gold: orange (3), orange (3), brown (×10) → 33 × 10 = 330 Ω, ±5%. A 1 kΩ resistor is brown–black–red–gold: brown (1), black (0), red (×100) → 10 × 100 = 1,000 Ω = 1 kΩ, ±5%.

Tip: if the bands are ambiguous (some colours are hard to tell apart in poor light), do not guess — set your multimeter to the ohms (Ω) range and measure the resistor directly. We will do exactly that below. Reading the bag label from a starter kit is the fastest method of all.

LED Polarity

An LED is a one-way street. Get the direction right and it lights; get it backwards and it simply stays dark (no harm done — an LED will not be damaged by being put in the wrong way round at these low voltages). There are three ways to spot which leg is which:

  • Leg length: the longer leg is the anode (+); the shorter leg is the cathode (−).
  • The flat edge: the plastic rim has a tiny flat on the cathode (−) side.
  • Inside the dome: the larger metal piece (the anvil) inside the plastic is the cathode (−).
Note: in every wiring project in this Part, current flows from a GPIO pin, through a 330 Ω resistor, into the LED's long leg (anode), out of the short leg (cathode), and back to GND. Memorise "long leg to the positive side" and you will rarely wire an LED backwards.

Using a Multimeter

A multimeter is the single most useful tool for testing parts — especially salvaged ones, where you cannot trust a label. It is a dial (or buttons) surrounded by settings, with a red probe and a black probe. Four settings cover almost everything you will do in this Part.

SettingSymbolWhat it does
Continuitysound-wave / 🔊beeps if the two probes are joined by an unbroken wire — tests cables, switches, solder joints
ResistanceΩ (omega)measures resistance in ohms — reads a resistor's value, or checks a coil/speaker
Diode test▷∣ (arrow + bar)checks diodes and LEDs for direction; lights an LED dimly when probes are the right way round
DC voltsV… (V with straight lines)measures direct-current voltage — battery level, a sensor's output, the Pi's 3.3 V/5 V pins

Continuity — is this wire alive?

Set the dial to continuity (the speaker/sound-wave symbol). Touch the two probes together first — it should beep, confirming the meter works. Now touch one probe to each end of a wire or each side of a switch: a beep means an unbroken connection; silence means a break. This is how you test a salvaged USB cable, a jumper wire, or whether a button actually closes when pressed.

Resistance — what value is this resistor?

Set the dial to a resistance range above your expected value (e.g. the 2 kΩ range for a 330 Ω resistor). Touch one probe to each leg — resistors have no polarity, so direction does not matter. The display shows the value; a 330 Ω resistor will read somewhere around 314–347 Ω (within the ±5% tolerance). This is the foolproof way to confirm bands you cannot quite read.

Diode test — which way round is this LED?

Set the dial to the diode symbol (▷ with a bar). Touch the red probe to the LED's long leg (anode) and the black probe to the short leg (cathode): the LED should glow faintly and the meter shows a forward voltage (roughly 1.5–3.5 V depending on colour). Swap the probes and it stays dark with no reading. This both proves the LED works and confirms its polarity — invaluable when a salvaged LED's legs have been trimmed to the same length.

DC volts — how much is this supplying?

Set the dial to DC volts (V with a solid and dashed line) on a range above what you expect (e.g. the 20 V range for a 9 V battery). Touch the red probe to the positive terminal and black to negative. A 1.5 V cell reading 0.9 V is nearly flat; a salvaged battery reading near 0 V is dead. You will also use this to confirm a Pi pin really is at 3.3 V before trusting it.

Careful: only ever measure low-voltage DC (batteries, sensor outputs, the Pi's pins) with a hobby multimeter. Never put the probes into a mains socket or onto mains wiring — household mains is lethal and well beyond what beginner gear and beginner skills should touch. If a part is connected to mains, it is off-limits (see the recycling section).

A Quick Python Refresher

Python is the main language of this whole course, so let us make sure the foundations are solid before the builds begin. If you have done the earlier chapters this will be a recap; if you are jumping in here, it is everything you need to follow along.

Two ways to run a program

You will run Python in two ways throughout this Part:

  1. In Thonny — the friendly editor preinstalled on Raspberry Pi OS. Open it from the Raspberry Pi menu → Programming → Thonny, type your code in the top pane, and click the green Run button (▶) or press F5. Output appears in the Shell at the bottom. Thonny is best while writing and debugging because you can run a line at a time and inspect variables.
  2. From the terminal with python3 file.py — this is how a "real" program is launched, and how your projects will eventually run on their own. Open a terminal, move to the folder holding your file, and run it.
python3 hello.py

python3: the command that starts the Python 3 interpreter. On Raspberry Pi OS always use python3 (and pip3), never bare python, to be sure you are on Python 3.

hello.py: the file to run. Python reads it top to bottom and executes each line. The file must be in your current folder, or you give its full path.

Variables, print() and f-strings

A variable is a named box that holds a value. print() displays things in the console. An f-string (a string prefixed with f) lets you drop variables straight into text inside {curly braces}.

name = "Maker"
leds = 3
print("Hello, " + name)
print(f"You have {leds} LEDs and {leds * 2} legs to wire.")

Line 1 — name = "Maker": creates a variable called name holding the text (a string) "Maker".

Line 2 — leds = 3: creates a variable holding the whole number (an integer) 3.

Line 3 — print("Hello, " + name): joins two strings with + and prints Hello, Maker.

Line 4 — the f-string: the f before the quotes lets you insert variables and even do maths inside the braces. {leds} becomes 3 and {leds * 2} is calculated to 6, printing You have 3 LEDs and 6 legs to wire. f-strings are the tidiest way to build messages, and you will use them constantly.

Functions

A function is a named, reusable block of code. You define it once with def and call it as often as you like. Functions can take inputs (called parameters) and hand back a result with return.

def greet(who):
    print(f"Welcome to the Maker Lab, {who}!")

def add(a, b):
    return a + b

greet("everyone")
total = add(2, 3)
print(total)

Lines 1–2 — def greet(who):: defines a function named greet that takes one parameter, who. The indented line is its body; it runs only when the function is called.

Lines 4–5 — def add(a, b): return a + b: defines add, which takes two parameters and returns their sum back to whoever called it.

Line 7 — greet("everyone"): calls greet, passing "everyone" in as who; it prints the welcome.

Line 8 — total = add(2, 3): calls add and stores the returned value (5) in total.

Line 9 — print(total): displays 5. Wrapping repeated steps in functions keeps your project programs short and readable.

import — borrowing other people's code

The import keyword pulls in a module or library — a bundle of ready-written code — so you do not have to write everything yourself. You will start nearly every project with imports.

import random
from gpiozero import LED

print(random.randint(1, 6))
led = LED(17)

Line 1 — import random: brings in Python's built-in random module; you then use its tools with the random. prefix.

Line 2 — from gpiozero import LED: imports just the LED object from the gpiozero library, so you can write LED(17) directly without a prefix. gpiozero is preinstalled on Bookworm and is the library you will use for every electronics build.

Line 4 — random.randint(1, 6): rolls a whole number from 1 to 6, like a die.

Line 5 — led = LED(17): creates an LED object on GPIO 17 — a taste of what is coming next chapter.

Installing Packages: the Bookworm Rule (PEP 668)

Most of the time you will import libraries that are already installed (gpiozero, random, time). Occasionally a project needs an extra one. On older systems you might have typed pip install something straight away — but on Raspberry Pi OS Bookworm that is deliberately blocked.

In plain words: PEP 668 is a Python rule that marks the system Python as "externally managed". It means: do not let pip scribble over the Python that the operating system itself depends on, because that can break your whole desktop. If you try a plain system-wide pip install on Bookworm, you will get an error: externally-managed-environment message — that is the rule protecting you, not a bug.

There are two correct ways to install a package on Bookworm:

Option 1 — use apt (simplest when a Pi package exists):

sudo apt install python3-gpiozero

sudo apt install python3-<name>: installs a Debian-packaged Python library system-wide, the operating-system-approved way. Many common libraries are available as python3-… packages (e.g. python3-gpiozero, python3-flask). This sidesteps PEP 668 entirely because apt, not pip, manages it.

Option 2 — make a virtual environment (a venv) and pip-install inside it:

python3 -m venv myenv
source myenv/bin/activate
pip install some-library

Line 1 — python3 -m venv myenv: creates a private, self-contained Python environment in a folder called myenv. Anything you install here is kept apart from the system Python, so it cannot break your desktop.

Line 2 — source myenv/bin/activate: switches your terminal into that environment. Your prompt gains a (myenv) prefix to remind you. Now python and pip point inside the venv.

Line 3 — pip install some-library: installs into the venv freely — no PEP 668 error, because you are no longer touching the system Python. Type deactivate to leave the venv later.

Careful: you may see advice online to "just add --break-system-packages" to force a system-wide pip install. The flag's own name is the warning: it can break the tools your Pi relies on. Prefer apt or a venv. We will use a venv properly in the final capstone chapter when we install Flask.

A First Taste of Bash

Python is not the only language living on your Pi. The black terminal window you type commands into is itself running a language called Bash. Every ls, cd or sudo apt install you type is a Bash command — and you can save a list of those commands into a file to run them all at once. That file is a Bash script.

In plain words: Bash is the language of the command line. A Bash script is a plain text file full of terminal commands that run top to bottom. It is perfect for gluing together jobs you would otherwise type by hand — backups, updates, launching your Python program at boot.

Here is a tiny script. Open Thonny or any text editor, type this, and save it as hello.sh:

#!/bin/bash
echo "Hello from Bash!"
echo "Today the Pi says:"
date

Line 1 — #!/bin/bash: the shebang. This special first line tells the system "run this file with the Bash program". Without it, the system would not know which language the file is written in.

Line 2 — echo "Hello from Bash!": echo is Bash's version of print() — it prints text to the terminal.

Line 3 — another echo: prints a second line of text.

Line 4 — date: runs the built-in date command, printing the current date and time. A Bash script is mostly just commands you could have typed yourself.

Before you can run a script with ./, you must mark the file as executable — give it permission to run as a program:

chmod +x hello.sh
./hello.sh

Line 1 — chmod +x hello.sh: chmod ("change mode") sets file permissions; +x adds the "executable" permission. You only need to do this once per script.

Line 2 — ./hello.sh: runs the script. The ./ means "the file in this current folder" — without it, the shell would search the system folders for a command called hello.sh and not find your file. The shebang then makes Bash interpret it.

Tip: a quick rule of thumb — reach for Bash when the job is mostly running other programs and shuffling files; reach for Python when the job involves real logic, maths, data, or talking to hardware. You will use both in this Part: Python for the projects, and the occasional Bash command to install or launch them.

The E-Waste Pile: Salvaging Safely

Here is the maker's open secret: a huge amount of the gear you need is sitting in cupboards, charity-shop "spares or repair" bins, and recycling skips. A dead printer is a bag of motors and rods. An old mouse is a tidy micro-switch and an LED. A broken toy is a speaker and a gearbox. Learning to harvest and test these parts is good for your wallet and the planet — but it comes with one absolutely firm rule.

Careful — read this before opening anything: the danger in old electronics is not the little 3.3 V and 5 V parts we play with. It is mains electricity and the large capacitors that store it. A capacitor can hold a lethal charge for a long time after the device is unplugged. Some devices must never be opened, full stop. The two-column figure below is your rule of thumb.

Dangerous — never open these

Careful — CRT TVs and monitors. The old, deep, glass-tubed sets. The picture tube runs at an anode voltage of 20,000–30,000 V, and internal capacitors can hold that charge for a long time after unplugging. The glass tube is also under vacuum and can implode into flying shards if cracked. Never open one. Recycle it whole.
Careful — microwave ovens. A microwave's high-voltage capacitor can store a lethal 4,000 V or more even when the oven is unplugged. People have been killed by them. Always treat that capacitor as charged. Never open a microwave oven — recycle it whole.
Careful — mains power supplies and adapters. Anything that plugs into the wall and converts mains down (PC power supplies, laptop bricks, plug-in chargers) contains large filter capacitors that store a dangerous charge on the mains side. Discard the whole adapter rather than opening it. The neat low-voltage USB cable hanging off a charger is fine to keep — just cut it free of the brick and never open the brick itself.
Careful — lithium-ion batteries. The rechargeable cells in phones, laptops and power banks will catch fire or explode if punctured, crushed, bent or short-circuited. Never cut into one, never deform a swollen one, and never throw them in normal bin or fire. Take them to a battery recycling point.
Note: the safe rule is simple — if it plugs into the mains wall socket, or has a big can-shaped capacitor, or a lithium battery, do not open it. Take it whole to a WEEE (Waste Electrical and Electronic Equipment) recycling point. Everything we build in this Part runs from the Pi's gentle 3.3 V/5 V or small batteries — there is no reason to ever go near mains.

Safe to harvest — the treasure list

Now the fun part. These devices are low-voltage, battery- or USB-powered, and full of useful parts. Always unplug them and remove any batteries first, but once that is done they are safe to open and explore:

  • Broken printers — a goldmine: stepper motors, DC motors, metal rods, gears, and optical/limit switches.
  • Mice and keyboards — tactile micro-switches (great buttons), LEDs, and a USB cable.
  • Old toys — small DC motors, speakers, gearboxes, and sometimes LEDs.
  • Dead routers and modems — indicator LEDs, buttons, connectors, and small heatsinks.
  • TV remotes — infrared (IR) LEDs and lovely rubber buttons.
  • Broken speakers and headphones — speaker drivers and thin wire.
  • Old USB cables — ready-made 5 V power leads.

How to harvest a component and test it

Most parts are either plugged in, screwed in, or soldered to a circuit board. Here is the routine:

  1. Make it safe. Unplug the device from any power and remove all batteries. Confirm there is no mains adapter still attached. Only safe-list devices, please.
  2. Open it up. Undo the screws (some hide under rubber feet or labels) and gently prise the case apart. Take a photo first so you remember how it goes together.
  3. Free the part. If it is held by a plug or screws, simply remove it. If it is soldered, you will need to desolder it: heat the joint with a soldering iron and lift the leg free, or use a solder sucker/braid to remove the molten solder. Work in a ventilated space and never touch the hot tip.
  4. Test it with your multimeter. Now prove the part actually works before you build with it:
    • a salvaged LED → use the diode test; red probe on the long leg should make it glow.
    • a switch or button → use continuity; it should beep only when pressed.
    • a resistor → use the resistance (Ω) range to read its value.
    • a speaker or motor coil → resistance should read a few ohms, not "open" (infinite) or "0" (a dead short).
    • a salvaged cable → continuity end-to-end to find which wire is which and that none is broken.
  5. Label and store it. A little box of tested, labelled parts is worth more than a drawer of mystery junk.
Careful: a soldering iron runs at around 350 °C — hot enough for a serious burn and to start a fire. Use a stand, keep it away from cables and skin, solder in a ventilated room, and let parts cool before handling. If you are under 16 or new to it, do desoldering with an adult nearby.

Make It Your Own

You have not wired anything yet, but you can already practise the skills that make every later build easier. Try these:

  • Sort your kit. Open your starter kit and identify every part using this chapter: name each resistor by its colour bands, find the long leg on each LED, and test which jumper-wire types you have.
  • Meter practice. Measure three resistors with the Ω range and check they match their bands. Diode-test an LED both ways round. Continuity-test a USB cable.
  • Write and run a Bash script. Make a hello.sh that prints a greeting and the date, chmod +x it, and run it with ./hello.sh.
  • Practise f-strings. Write a short Python program that stores your name and the number of parts in your kit, then prints a tidy summary line with an f-string.
  • Start a salvage box. Find one safe-list dead gadget (an old mouse is perfect), open it, harvest the micro-switch and any LED, test them, and label them. You have just earned your first salvaged parts.
