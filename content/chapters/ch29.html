A houseplant cannot tell you it is thirsty — but your Pi can listen on its behalf. In this chapter you will build a plant monitor that checks the soil and the air, then quietly writes a timestamped row to a file every few minutes, building up a history you can read back to find the driest moment of the day. You will learn how to open, write and read files, save tidy data with the CSV module, organise readings as a list of dictionaries, stamp each one with the date and time, and finish with a function that prints a daily summary. On the electronics side you will finally understand the deep difference between digital and analog — and why the Pi, alone among hobby boards, has no way to read an analog voltage directly.

What You'll Build & Learn

Your finished project is a data logger: every N minutes it reads a soil-moisture sensor and a temperature/humidity sensor, appends a timestamped row to a CSV file, and — at the end of a run — reads the whole file back to print a tidy summary (driest reading, warmest reading, and so on). It is the foundation of every weather station, fitness tracker and laboratory instrument ever made: sense, record, review.

Digital vs Analog & the Missing ADC

This is the single most important electronics idea in the chapter, and it shapes every sensor choice you make on a Pi.

In plain words: A digital signal has only two states — ON or OFF, HIGH or LOW, 1 or 0. An analog signal varies smoothly through every value in between, like a dimmer dial rather than a light switch.

A push button is digital: pressed or not pressed. A spinning volume knob is analog: it can sit at any point from fully off to fully on. Many real-world quantities — temperature, light level, the wetness of soil — are naturally analog: they slide smoothly up and down.

Careful — the key Pi fact: The Raspberry Pi has no analog input. It has no ADC (Analogue-to-Digital Converter) at all. Every one of its GPIO pins is purely digital — it can only ever tell whether a voltage is below or above the threshold between LOW and HIGH. This surprises people coming from boards like the Arduino, which do have analog inputs. So if a sensor produces a smoothly-varying analog voltage (like a cheap resistive soil probe), the Pi cannot read it directly. You must either choose a sensor that gives a digital output, or add an external ADC chip.
In plain words: An ADC (Analogue-to-Digital Converter) is a chip that measures an analog voltage and reports it as a number a computer can understand. The Pi has none built in; the Arduino does. This is the single biggest difference beginners trip over.

Your three options on a Pi

Approach How it works Best for
Digital-output sensor The sensor has its own little chip that decides "wet/dry" or "bright/dark" and gives a clean HIGH/LOW the Pi reads directly with DigitalInputDevice or Button. Simple yes/no questions — is the soil dry? Easiest by far.
Digital data sensor (e.g. DHT22, or I²C) The sensor measures internally and sends the Pi a stream of digital bits encoding the actual number (temperature, humidity). Reading true values over one or two wires using a driver library.
External ADC (e.g. MCP3008 over SPI) An add-on chip measures the analog voltage and tells the Pi the number digitally. When you must read a genuinely analog sensor (a real moisture level, a light dependent resistor).

For our plant monitor we use the two friendliest options: a digital capacitive soil-moisture sensor for "wet or dry", and a DHT22 for temperature and humidity. We will look at the MCP3008 briefly at the end for when you outgrow them.

Choosing the Sensors

The soil-moisture sensor — choose capacitive, not resistive

Cheap soil sensors come in two flavours, and the difference matters:

  • Resistive probes pass a current through the soil between two metal prongs. They are analog (so the Pi cannot read them without an ADC) and, worse, the prongs corrode quickly because of the current.
  • Capacitive probes sense moisture without passing current into the soil, so they last far longer. Crucially, the common capacitive modules include a small comparator chip and a sensitivity screw, giving a clean digital DO (digital out) pin: HIGH when dry, LOW when wet (or the reverse, depending on the board). That digital pin plugs straight into a Pi GPIO — no ADC needed.
Tip: Capacitive sensors are the right choice for a Pi because their digital output works without an ADC and they don't rot in the pot. Turn the little screw on the board to set the wet/dry threshold: water the plant, then adjust until the onboard LED just switches as the soil dries.

We read its digital DO pin with gpiozero's DigitalInputDevice:

from gpiozero import DigitalInputDevice

# DO pin of the capacitive sensor on GPIO 17. active_state=False means
# the sensor pulls the line LOW when it detects moisture.
soil = DigitalInputDevice(17, pull_up=None, active_state=False)

if soil.value == 1:
    print("Soil is WET")
else:
    print("Soil is DRY")

Line 1 — from gpiozero import DigitalInputDevice: DigitalInputDevice is the general-purpose object for reading any HIGH/LOW input — perfect for a sensor whose DO pin is already digital.

Line 5 — soil = DigitalInputDevice(17, pull_up=None, active_state=False): Reads GPIO 17. The sensor drives the line itself, so we set pull_up=None (no internal resistor) and tell gpiozero that the "active" (detected) state is a LOW line with active_state=False. If your board reads the opposite way, swap to active_state=True.

Lines 7–10 — if soil.value == 1:: soil.value is 1 when the sensor is active (moisture detected) and 0 otherwise — a clean digital read, no ADC in sight.

The DHT22 — and an honest word about the Pi 5

The DHT22 (and its cheaper cousin the DHT11) measures temperature and humidity and sends the Pi a digital data stream on a single signal wire. It is popular and inexpensive — but on the Raspberry Pi 5 the software story is genuinely awkward, so let's be honest about it.

Careful — outdated tutorials will fail on the Pi 5. Two libraries you will find all over the internet do not work on the Pi 5: the old Adafruit_DHT library is archived and fails on Bookworm with Python 3.11; and the pigpio daemon (which some DHT guides rely on) is not supported on the Pi 5 either. If a tutorial tells you to pip install Adafruit_DHT or to start pigpiod, stop — it will not work.

The combination that does work today is the current CircuitPython stack: adafruit-circuitpython-dht together with a recent adafruit-blinka (which routes GPIO through lgpio on the Pi 5). Because of PEP 668 on Bookworm, install them inside a virtual environment:

python3 -m venv plantenv
source plantenv/bin/activate
pip install adafruit-circuitpython-dht adafruit-blinka

Line 1 — python3 -m venv plantenv: Creates a virtual environment (an isolated, private set of Python packages) in a folder called plantenv. This sidesteps Bookworm's "externally-managed-environment" block on system-wide pip.

Line 2 — source plantenv/bin/activate: Switches your terminal into that environment. Your prompt gains a (plantenv) prefix. Run your script from this same activated terminal.

Line 3 — pip install ...: Installs the two libraries that actually work on the Pi 5. adafruit-blinka is the glue that lets CircuitPython libraries talk to the Pi's hardware via lgpio.

Reading it then looks like this — note that the DHT22 is fussy and sometimes returns a checksum error, so we catch it and try again:

import board
import adafruit_dht

dht = adafruit_dht.DHT22(board.D4)   # data pin on GPIO 4

def read_temp_humidity():
    """Return (temperature_c, humidity_pct), retrying on checksum errors."""
    for attempt in range(5):
        try:
            return dht.temperature, dht.humidity
        except RuntimeError:
            sleep(2)   # a normal hiccup — wait and retry
    return None, None

Lines 1–2 — imports: board names the Pi's pins the CircuitPython way (board.D4 = GPIO 4); adafruit_dht is the working DHT driver.

Line 4 — dht = adafruit_dht.DHT22(board.D4): Creates the sensor object on GPIO 4.

Line 6 — def read_temp_humidity():: A function that returns a pair of values: the temperature in °C and the humidity as a percentage.

Lines 8–13 — the retry loop: The DHT22 occasionally fails its internal checksum and raises a RuntimeError. Rather than crash, we try up to five times, pausing two seconds between attempts. If every attempt fails, we return None, None so the caller can skip that reading.

Tip: If you want a rock-solid reading with no checksum drama, an I²C sensor such as the SHT4x or BME280 is the most reliable teaching choice on the Pi 5. It connects to just the two I²C pins (SDA/SCL), needs no divider, and its CircuitPython library is stable. The logging code in this chapter works the same whichever sensor you read from — only the read_temp_humidity() function changes.
Note: If you do not have a DHT22 yet, you can still build and learn the whole logger: replace read_temp_humidity() with a version that returns made-up numbers (e.g. return 21.4, 55.0). Every file, CSV and summary idea works identically with stand-in data.

Files, CSV & Lists of Dictionaries

Before wiring anything, let's master the data side with small examples. These are the Python fundamentals at the heart of the project.

Opening and writing a file

with open("hello.txt", "w") as f:
    f.write("Hello, plant!\n")
    f.write("This is line two.\n")

Line 1 — with open("hello.txt", "w") as f:: open() opens (and here creates) a file. The "w" means "write mode" — it starts the file fresh, erasing any old contents. The with keyword is the safe way to open files: it automatically closes the file when the indented block ends, even if something goes wrong. f is our handle to the open file.

Lines 2–3 — f.write(...): Writes text into the file. \n is a newline character, so each write ends up on its own line.

In plain words: The three common file modes are "w" (write — erases first), "a" (append — adds to the end, keeping what's there), and "r" (read — look but don't change). A logger uses "a" so each new reading is added without wiping the history.

Reading a file back

with open("hello.txt", "r") as f:
    for line in f:
        print(line.strip())

Line 1 — open(..., "r"): Opens the file in read mode.

Line 2 — for line in f:: Looping over an open file hands you one line at a time — a tidy, memory-friendly way to read even huge files.

Line 3 — print(line.strip()): .strip() removes the trailing newline so we don't print blank gaps between lines.

A list of dictionaries

A dictionary stores named values — perfect for one reading with several fields. A list of dictionaries is the natural shape for many such readings.

readings = [
    {"time": "09:00", "moisture": "wet", "temp": 20.1},
    {"time": "09:05", "moisture": "wet", "temp": 20.4},
    {"time": "09:10", "moisture": "dry", "temp": 21.0},
]

for r in readings:
    print(r["time"], "->", r["temp"], "C")

# Find the warmest reading by scanning the list
warmest = readings[0]
for r in readings:
    if r["temp"] > warmest["temp"]:
        warmest = r
print("Warmest:", warmest["temp"], "C at", warmest["time"])

Lines 1–5 — readings = [ ... ]: A list (square brackets) whose items are dictionaries (curly braces). Each dictionary holds named fields: "time", "moisture", "temp". This is exactly how we'll hold our logged data in memory.

Lines 7–8 — looping over the list: Each r is one dictionary; r["temp"] looks up that reading's temperature by name.

Lines 11–15 — finding the maximum: Start by assuming the first reading is warmest, then loop through and keep any reading that beats it. By the end, warmest holds the dictionary with the highest temp — the classic "find the max" pattern you'll reuse on the logged file.

The CSV module

A CSV (Comma-Separated Values) file is the universal format for tables of data — every spreadsheet program opens it. Python's built-in csv module writes and reads it cleanly, handling commas and quoting for you. csv.DictWriter and csv.DictReader pair beautifully with our list of dictionaries.

import csv

rows = [
    {"time": "09:00", "moisture": "wet", "temp": 20.1},
    {"time": "09:05", "moisture": "dry", "temp": 21.0},
]

with open("demo.csv", "w", newline="") as f:
    writer = csv.DictWriter(f, fieldnames=["time", "moisture", "temp"])
    writer.writeheader()
    writer.writerows(rows)

Line 1 — import csv: Brings in Python's built-in CSV tools — nothing to install.

Line 8 — open("demo.csv", "w", newline=""): Opens the CSV for writing. The newline="" is the recommended setting for the csv module so it controls line endings itself (this avoids blank lines on some systems).

Line 9 — csv.DictWriter(f, fieldnames=[...]): Creates a writer that maps dictionary keys to columns. fieldnames sets the column order.

Line 10 — writer.writeheader(): Writes the first row of column names (time,moisture,temp).

Line 11 — writer.writerows(rows): Writes every dictionary in our list as its own row. To read it back, csv.DictReader turns each row back into a dictionary.

Timestamps with datetime

from datetime import datetime

now = datetime.now()
print(now.strftime("%Y-%m-%d %H:%M:%S"))   # e.g. 2026-06-23 14:05:09

Line 1 — from datetime import datetime: Imports the datetime object, which represents a moment in time.

Line 3 — now = datetime.now(): Captures the current date and time right now.

Line 4 — now.strftime("%Y-%m-%d %H:%M:%S"): Formats the moment as readable text: four-digit year, month, day, then hours, minutes, seconds. Putting a timestamp on every reading is what turns a pile of numbers into a history you can chart.

Parts & Wiring

What you need

  • A Raspberry Pi 5 running Raspberry Pi OS Bookworm
  • 1 × capacitive soil-moisture sensor with a digital DO output
  • 1 × DHT22 temperature/humidity sensor (or an I²C SHT4x/BME280, or just use stand-in numbers)
  • 1 × 10 kΩ resistor (a pull-up for the DHT22 data line, if your DHT22 module doesn't already include one)
  • 1 × breadboard and a handful of jumper wires
Careful — power off first. Shut your Pi down fully (Raspberry Pi menu → Shutdown), wait for the green light to go out, and unplug the power before wiring. Both sensors here run from 3.3 V and their data/DO lines are 3.3 V logic, so — unlike the ultrasonic sensor in the last chapter — no voltage divider is needed. Still observe the golden rule: GPIO is 3.3 V logic, and you must never feed a higher voltage into a GPIO input. Re-connect power only after checking your wiring.

Wiring diagram

Connection table

From (component) To (Pi pin) Via Notes
Soil sensor VCC Pin 1 (3.3 V) direct Capacitive modules run happily at 3.3 V
Soil sensor DO (digital out) Pin 11 (GPIO 17) direct Already digital 3.3 V — no divider
Soil sensor GND Pin 9 (GND) common GND rail Shared with the DHT22
DHT22 + (VCC) Pin 17 (3.3 V) direct 3.3 V supply
DHT22 DATA Pin 7 (GPIO 4) 10 kΩ pull-up to 3.3 V Many DHT22 modules already include the pull-up
DHT22 − (GND) Pin 9 (GND) common GND rail Shared ground
Note: A bare 3-pin DHT22 needs the 10 kΩ pull-up resistor between its DATA line and 3.3 V. The common blue or white DHT22 modules on a small breakout board usually have this resistor built in, so you can skip it — check your board.

Building the Data Logger

Now we assemble the full program. It reads both sensors, appends a timestamped row to a CSV, repeats every N minutes, and prints a daily summary read back from the file. To keep it tidy we put the appending in its own logging function.

Open a new file in Thonny, type the program, and save it as plant_logger.py. (Run it from your activated plantenv terminal if you are using a real DHT22.)

import csv
import os
from time import sleep
from datetime import datetime
from gpiozero import DigitalInputDevice

# --- sensor objects ---
soil = DigitalInputDevice(17, pull_up=None, active_state=False)

# If you have a real DHT22, uncomment these two lines and the body of
# read_temp_humidity() below (and run inside your venv):
# import board, adafruit_dht
# dht = adafruit_dht.DHT22(board.D4)

CSV_FILE = "plant_log.csv"
FIELDS = ["timestamp", "moisture", "temp_c", "humidity_pct"]
SAMPLE_MINUTES = 5     # how often to take a reading


def read_moisture():
    """Return 'wet' or 'dry' from the digital soil sensor."""
    return "wet" if soil.value == 1 else "dry"


def read_temp_humidity():
    """Return (temperature_c, humidity_pct). Replace body with real DHT22 code."""
    # --- real DHT22 version (needs the venv libraries) ---
    # for _ in range(5):
    #     try:
    #         return round(dht.temperature, 1), round(dht.humidity, 1)
    #     except RuntimeError:
    #         sleep(2)
    # return None, None
    # --- stand-in version so the logger runs without a DHT22 ---
    return 21.4, 55.0


def log_reading():
    """Take one reading and append it as a timestamped row to the CSV."""
    temp_c, humidity = read_temp_humidity()
    row = {
        "timestamp": datetime.now().strftime("%Y-%m-%d %H:%M:%S"),
        "moisture": read_moisture(),
        "temp_c": temp_c,
        "humidity_pct": humidity,
    }

    file_is_new = not os.path.exists(CSV_FILE)
    with open(CSV_FILE, "a", newline="") as f:
        writer = csv.DictWriter(f, fieldnames=FIELDS)
        if file_is_new:
            writer.writeheader()
        writer.writerow(row)

    print("Logged:", row)
    return row


def print_summary():
    """Read the whole CSV back and print the day's extremes."""
    with open(CSV_FILE, "r", newline="") as f:
        rows = list(csv.DictReader(f))

    if not rows:
        print("No data yet.")
        return

    temps = [float(r["temp_c"]) for r in rows if r["temp_c"]]
    dry_count = sum(1 for r in rows if r["moisture"] == "dry")

    print("\n----- Daily summary -----")
    print(f"Readings logged : {len(rows)}")
    if temps:
        print(f"Warmest         : {max(temps)} C")
        print(f"Coolest         : {min(temps)} C")
    print(f"Times soil dry  : {dry_count}")
    print("-------------------------\n")


print("Plant logger started. Press Ctrl+C to stop and see the summary.")
try:
    while True:
        log_reading()
        sleep(SAMPLE_MINUTES * 60)
except KeyboardInterrupt:
    print("\nStopping...")
    print_summary()

Lines 1–5 — imports: csv for the file format, os to check whether the file already exists, sleep for the timer, datetime for timestamps, and DigitalInputDevice to read the soil sensor.

Line 8 — soil = DigitalInputDevice(17, ...): The soil-moisture object on GPIO 17, exactly as in the earlier example.

Lines 15–17 — settings: CSV_FILE names our log file; FIELDS lists the column names in order; SAMPLE_MINUTES sets how often we take a reading. Change this one number to sample faster or slower.

Lines 20–22 — read_moisture(): A function returning the word "wet" or "dry" based on the digital soil reading — mapping a HIGH/LOW into a human label.

Lines 25–35 — read_temp_humidity(): Returns a temperature and humidity pair. The commented block is the real DHT22 code (with its checksum retry loop); the active line returns stand-in numbers so you can run the logger even without the sensor wired.

Lines 38–56 — log_reading() — the logging function: This is the heart of the project. It builds a dictionary (row) holding a fresh timestamp plus the three readings, then appends it to the CSV. os.path.exists(CSV_FILE) tells us if the file is brand new; if so, we write the header row first. Opening in "a" (append) mode means every call adds a row without wiping the history. It returns the row in case the caller wants it.

Lines 59–78 — print_summary() — reading data back: Opens the CSV in read mode and uses csv.DictReader to load every row back into a list of dictionaries. The line temps = [float(r["temp_c"]) for r in rows if r["temp_c"]] is a list comprehension that pulls out all the temperatures as numbers. Then max(temps) and min(temps) find the warmest and coolest — the "find min/max by reading data back" goal. dry_count counts how many readings found dry soil.

Lines 81–88 — the main loop: Prints a start message, then loops forever: take one reading (log_reading()), then sleep for SAMPLE_MINUTES × 60 seconds before the next. Pressing Ctrl+C raises KeyboardInterrupt, which we catch to print the summary on the way out — a tidy finish.

Tip: While testing, set SAMPLE_MINUTES to a fraction such as 0.1 (six seconds) so you don't have to wait five minutes between rows. Set it back to 5 (or higher) for real plant monitoring, where things change slowly.

Running It

  2. Reconnect power and boot the Pi.
  3. If using a real DHT22, activate your venv first: in a terminal run source plantenv/bin/activate, then start Thonny from that same terminal so it can see the installed libraries. With stand-in numbers you can just run from Thonny directly.

A Look at True Analog: the MCP3008

Our capacitive sensor gives a simple wet/dry answer, which is plenty for watering a plant. But what if you wanted the exact moisture percentage, or to read a light-dependent resistor, or any genuinely analog sensor? Then you need to give the Pi the ADC it lacks. The classic, cheap choice is the MCP3008: an 8-channel ADC chip that talks to the Pi over the SPI bus.

In plain words: SPI (Serial Peripheral Interface) is a way for the Pi to chat with add-on chips over a few dedicated pins. The MCP3008 listens on SPI, measures the analog voltage on one of its 8 input channels, and reports back a number — giving the Pi the analog reading it cannot take itself.

Once SPI is enabled (in raspi-config) and the chip is wired to the SPI pins, gpiozero makes it almost magically simple:

from gpiozero import MCP3008

# An analog moisture probe on channel 0 of the MCP3008
moisture = MCP3008(channel=0)

while True:
    level = moisture.value          # a float from 0.0 to 1.0
    print(round(level * 100), "% wet")
    sleep(2)

Line 1 — from gpiozero import MCP3008: gpiozero has built-in support for this ADC chip — no separate driver needed.

Line 4 — moisture = MCP3008(channel=0): Reads channel 0 of the chip (it has 8, numbered 0–7).

Line 7 — level = moisture.value: Returns the analog reading as a number from 0.0 (0 V) to 1.0 (3.3 V) — true analog, brought into the digital Pi by the ADC. (There is also .voltage, which gives the reading in volts.)

Line 8 — print(round(level * 100), "% wet"): Scales the 0–1 value into a friendly 0–100% reading.

To log analog moisture instead of wet/dry, you would simply swap read_moisture() to return round(moisture.value * 100) — the rest of the logger, CSV and summary code stays exactly the same. That is the payoff of putting each job in its own small function.

Make It Your Own

  • Email or beep when the soil is dry. Inside log_reading(), if the moisture is "dry", switch on a buzzer (from Chapter 28) or flash an LED to nag you to water the plant.
  • Add more columns. Because each reading is a dictionary, adding a light sensor is as simple as putting a new key in row and a new name in FIELDS. The CSV grows a column automatically.
  • Run it automatically at boot. Once you're happy, set the logger to start whenever the Pi powers on using a systemd service or a cron job, so your plant is monitored around the clock with no screen needed.
  • Chart your data. Open plant_log.csv in LibreOffice Calc and make a line graph of temperature over time — or, looking ahead to the capstone, serve the latest reading to a web page you can check from your phone.
Note: Logging to a file is the same technique professionals use for weather stations and lab equipment. See the gpiozero docs at gpiozero.readthedocs.io for MCP3008, DigitalInputDevice and more, and the Python docs for the full power of the csv and datetime modules.

Key terms

  • Digital signal - a value that is only ever HIGH or LOW (1 or 0); every Pi GPIO pin is digital.
  • Analog signal - a value that varies smoothly through every level in between.
  • ADC - Analogue-to-Digital Converter; a chip that turns an analog voltage into a number. The Pi has none built in.
  • Capacitive soil sensor - a moisture sensor that gives a clean digital wet/dry output and does not corrode; ideal for the ADC-less Pi.
  • DigitalInputDevice - the gpiozero object for reading any HIGH/LOW input pin.
  • DHT22 - a digital temperature/humidity sensor; on the Pi 5 use adafruit_dht + adafruit-blinka (legacy Adafruit_DHT and pigpio do not work).
  • MCP3008 - an 8-channel ADC chip read over SPI; gpiozero's MCP3008(channel=0) gives .value 0–1.
  • File mode - "w" write (erases), "a" append (adds), "r" read.
  • with open(...) - the safe way to open a file; it closes automatically when the block ends.
  • CSV - Comma-Separated Values; a universal table format handled by Python's csv module.
  • List of dictionaries - the natural shape for many readings, each with named fields.
  • datetime / strftime - tools for capturing the current moment and formatting it as a timestamp string.

Chapter summary

  • A digital signal is only ever HIGH or LOW; an analog signal varies smoothly. The Raspberry Pi has no ADC, so every GPIO pin can only read digital signals.
  • To sense analog quantities you must pick a sensor with a digital output (like a capacitive soil sensor) or add an external ADC such as the MCP3008 over SPI.
  • The capacitive soil sensor's digital DO pin reads cleanly with DigitalInputDevice; no voltage divider is needed because it is already 3.3 V logic.
  • On the Pi 5, the legacy Adafruit_DHT and pigpio DHT methods fail; use the current adafruit_dht + adafruit-blinka stack (in a venv) and catch occasional checksum errors — or prefer a stable I²C sensor.
  • Files open with open(); use with for safety, "a" mode to append log rows, and "r" to read them back.
  • The csv module's DictWriter and DictReader pair perfectly with a list of dictionaries, the natural shape for timestamped readings.
  • Every reading is stamped with a datetime timestamp; reading the CSV back lets you scan for the min and max — the warmest and coolest moments of the day.
  • Putting each job in its own small function (read, log, summarise) means swapping in a different sensor — even an analog one via the MCP3008 — changes almost nothing else.
