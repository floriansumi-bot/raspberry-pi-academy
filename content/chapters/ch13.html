Your Raspberry Pi 5 is powerful enough to be a proper home media centre — one that plays films in crystal-clear 4K, streams your music collection to every room, and lets you control everything from your phone or TV remote. This chapter walks you through your options, gives you a clear recommendation, and then takes you step by step through a full working setup that any beginner can complete.

What Are Your Options?

There are four main approaches to turning your Pi into a media box. They suit different situations, so read through them before picking one.

Option What it is Best for Cost
Kodi A media-player app you install on top of Raspberry Pi OS. Your Pi still runs a normal desktop. People who want a general-purpose Pi that also plays media stored locally on a USB drive. Free
LibreELEC A tiny, stripped-down operating system that is just Kodi — nothing else. Very fast to boot and very simple. A Pi that will only ever be a media box plugged into the TV. Free
Jellyfin A media server that runs on your Pi and streams your film/music library to phones, tablets, smart TVs, and browsers around the house. People with a large collection of films or music who want to watch on multiple devices. Free & open source
Plex Similar to Jellyfin but partly commercial. The free tier works well; some features (such as offline downloads and live TV) need a paid "Plex Pass". People who want a polished interface and do not mind optional paid features. Free (core); paid optional
In plain words: A media server is software that sits on one computer (your Pi) and "serves" — sends — your films and music to other devices over your home Wi-Fi or network, just like a tiny Netflix running in your own house.

Which Should You Choose?

Here is a plain-English recommendation:

  • Use LibreELEC if you want a dedicated box that boots straight to a TV-friendly media player, with big remote-friendly menus. You flash it to your microSD card exactly like Raspberry Pi OS (using Raspberry Pi Imager), and the Pi becomes a media box. You lose the desktop, but everything is simpler. Visit libreelec.tv for the download.
  • Use Jellyfin if you want to keep Raspberry Pi OS (so your Pi stays useful for everything else) and you have a library of films, TV episodes, or music you want to watch on any screen in the house. This chapter follows this path end to end.
  • Use Kodi on Raspberry Pi OS if you want a local media player without a server — just install it with one command and point it at your USB drive. Quick instructions are at the end of this chapter if you prefer this lighter option.
Note: LibreELEC replaces Raspberry Pi OS entirely on the microSD card. If you flash LibreELEC, you will need a second microSD card if you ever want to go back to your normal Pi desktop. Keep that in mind before flashing.

A Word on Media You Can Play

Careful: Only use Jellyfin, Kodi, or any media software with films, music, and TV shows that you own or have the legal right to play. Downloading copyrighted content without paying for it is illegal in most countries, including the UK and EU. This guide assumes you have your own DVD/Blu-ray rips, home videos, or legally downloaded files.

Full Worked Path: Jellyfin on Raspberry Pi OS

We will install Jellyfin on your Pi, point it at a USB drive full of films, and then watch from your phone or TV. This takes about 20–30 minutes, most of which is waiting for downloads.

What You Will Need

  • Your Pi 5 running Raspberry Pi OS (Bookworm, 64-bit) and connected to your home network.
  • A USB drive (or USB hard drive) containing your media files — films, TV shows, music.
  • A phone, tablet, or smart TV on the same Wi-Fi network as your Pi.
  • An internet connection for the Pi to download Jellyfin.

Step 1 — Plug In Your USB Drive and Check It Is Recognised

Plug your USB drive into one of the blue USB 3.0 ports on the Pi (the blue-coloured connectors are USB 3.0 — faster than the black USB 2.0 ports, which matters for large video files).

Open a Terminal on your Pi (click the black screen icon on the taskbar, or press Ctrl+Alt+T). Now check which devices are connected:

lsblk -o NAME,SIZE,MOUNTPOINT,LABEL,FSTYPE

lsblk lists every storage device connected to your Pi. The flags after -o choose which columns to display: the device name, its size, where it is mounted (accessible), its human-readable label, and its filesystem type. Look for a line like sda1 with a size matching your USB drive. The MOUNTPOINT column shows the path you use to reach its files — for example /media/pi/MOVIES. The FSTYPE column tells you whether the drive is formatted as vfat (FAT32), exfat, ntfs, or ext4 — make a note of this, as you will need it later.

Tip: If the MOUNTPOINT column is empty for your drive, Raspberry Pi OS has not yet mounted it automatically. Usually it does so within a few seconds of plugging in. Wait a moment and run the command again. You can also open the File Manager (the folder icon on the taskbar) — this often triggers the automatic mount.

Make a note of the mountpoint path and the filesystem type. We will use both in later steps. For the rest of this chapter, examples use the path /media/pi/MOVIES — substitute your actual path wherever you see that.

Step 2 — Install Support for Windows-Formatted Drives (if needed)

Most USB drives sold ready-to-use come formatted as exFAT or NTFS — the formats Windows uses. Raspberry Pi OS Bookworm can read these, but only if the right support packages are installed. Check your filesystem type from Step 1 and install the right one:

If your drive is exFAT (most common for drives under 2 TB bought recently):

sudo apt install exfatprogs -y

Installs exfatprogs, the modern tool that lets Raspberry Pi OS read and write exFAT drives. Without this, an exFAT drive may fail to mount or Jellyfin may report errors. The -y flag confirms installation automatically.

If your drive is NTFS (common for older drives or ones formatted by Windows 10/11):

sudo apt install ntfs-3g -y

Installs ntfs-3g, the driver that allows full read and write access to NTFS drives on Linux. Without this, NTFS drives mount in read-only mode or may not mount at all. -y confirms the installation.

Note: If your drive is already formatted as ext4 (Linux's own format) or vfat (FAT32), no extra package is needed — both are supported out of the box.

Step 3 — Update Your Pi

Before installing anything new, bring your Pi up to date:

sudo apt update && sudo apt upgrade -y

sudo apt update refreshes the list of available software packages from the internet. && means "only run the next command if the first one succeeded without errors". sudo apt upgrade -y then installs all available updates; the -y flag answers "yes" automatically so you do not have to confirm each update. This can take a few minutes.

Step 4 — Install Jellyfin from Its Official Repository

In plain words: A repository (often shortened to "repo") is an online library of software packages. Adding Jellyfin's official repository means your Pi knows where to find the Jellyfin software and how to verify it is genuine. Future updates then happen automatically alongside your normal system updates — you do not have to re-download or re-install Jellyfin manually.

First, make sure the curl download tool is installed:

sudo apt install curl -y

Installs curl, a small tool for downloading files from the internet. It is often already present on Raspberry Pi OS, but this command ensures it is there before the next step relies on it.

Now run the official Jellyfin installer script:

curl -fsSL https://repo.jellyfin.org/install-debuntu.sh | sudo bash

This single command does the entire Jellyfin setup for you. Breaking it down: curl -fsSL downloads a file from the internet silently and stops cleanly if there is an error. The URL points to the official Jellyfin installer script on repo.jellyfin.org. The | (pipe) symbol passes the downloaded script directly to sudo bash, which runs it with administrator permission. The script automatically detects that your Pi 5 is a 64-bit ARM device (arm64), adds Jellyfin's repository to your Pi, imports a security key so your Pi can verify the software is genuine, and then installs Jellyfin. A lot of text will scroll past — that is normal. Wait for the prompt to return before moving on.

Note: Running a script downloaded from the internet with sudo is worth doing thoughtfully. Here we are using the official URL published by the Jellyfin project itself (repo.jellyfin.org). If you prefer to review what the script does before running it, you can open that URL in a browser and read it first — it is a plain text file.

Step 5 — Start Jellyfin and Make It Start Automatically at Boot

sudo systemctl enable --now jellyfin

systemctl is the tool that manages background services on Raspberry Pi OS. enable registers Jellyfin to start automatically every time the Pi boots up. --now also starts it immediately right now, without needing a reboot. jellyfin is the name of the service. After this command, Jellyfin is running in the background.

Confirm that Jellyfin is running:

sudo systemctl status jellyfin

Shows the current status of the Jellyfin background service. Look for the words active (running) in green near the top. If you see that, everything is working correctly. Press q to exit the status view and return to the normal command prompt.

Tip: If Jellyfin shows as "failed" rather than "active (running)", try rebooting the Pi (sudo reboot) and checking the status again. If it still fails, run sudo journalctl -u jellyfin -n 50 to see the last 50 lines of error messages — copy them into a web search to find a solution.

Step 6 — Find Your Pi's IP Address

To reach Jellyfin from other devices on your home network, you need to know your Pi's address. Run:

hostname -I

Prints all network addresses assigned to your Pi. You will see one or two numbers such as 192.168.1.42. The first number shown is your Pi's address on your home network (its Wi-Fi or Ethernet address). Write it down — you will type it into a browser on other devices shortly.

In plain words: An IP address is your Pi's "house number" on the home network. Any device on the same Wi-Fi (phone, laptop, tablet, smart TV) can reach your Pi by typing this number into a web browser, just like typing a web address.

Step 7 — Open the Jellyfin Setup Wizard

On your Pi (or on any device on the same network), open a web browser and type the following address into the address bar:

http://192.168.1.42:8096

Replace 192.168.1.42 with your Pi's actual IP address that you noted in Step 6. The :8096 part is the port number — think of it as a door number within the Pi's address. Jellyfin listens on port 8096 by default. Because Raspberry Pi OS does not have a firewall enabled by default, this port is immediately reachable from any device on your home network.

In plain words: A port number is a number added after an IP address (like :8096) that tells your computer which specific service to connect to. Just as a building can have many flats, one computer can run many services — each listens on its own port number.

You should see the Jellyfin welcome screen. Follow the on-screen wizard:

  1. Choose your display language and click Next.
  2. Create an admin username and password. Write these down somewhere safe — you will need them to log in and manage Jellyfin. Do not use a password you use anywhere else.
  3. On the Set up your media libraries screen, click Add Media Library.
  4. Choose a Content type from the drop-down: for films choose Movies; for TV series choose Shows; for music choose Music.
  5. Click the + button next to Folders and type (or browse to) the path to your USB drive — for example /media/pi/MOVIES. Make sure the path matches exactly what you saw in Step 1.
  6. Click OK, then Next. Jellyfin will scan the folder and download artwork, descriptions, and ratings for your films automatically. This may take several minutes for a large library — be patient.
  7. Complete the rest of the wizard and click Finish. You will be taken to the Jellyfin home screen.
Tip: Jellyfin works best when your film files are named clearly — for example Inception (2010).mkv or The Grand Budapest Hotel (2014).mp4. Including the year in brackets helps Jellyfin identify the correct film and download the right artwork. For TV shows, use a folder structure like Breaking Bad/Season 1/Breaking Bad S01E01.mkv. If Jellyfin cannot match a file, rename it and trigger a library rescan from the Dashboard.

Step 8 — Watch Something

You can now watch from almost any device on your home network.

Watching from a web browser (any device)

Open a browser on your phone, laptop, or tablet and go to http://192.168.1.42:8096. Log in with the username and password you created. Click any film and press play. No app is needed — the web browser is enough.

Watching from a phone or tablet (Jellyfin app)

Search for Jellyfin in the App Store (iPhone/iPad) or Google Play Store (Android). Install the free official Jellyfin app, tap Add Server, and enter http://192.168.1.42:8096. Log in and your library appears. The app also lets you control playback on a TV and download films for offline viewing.

Watching on a Smart TV

Many smart TVs (Samsung, LG, Android TV, Amazon Fire TV, Chromecast with Google TV) have a Jellyfin app in their app store. Search for "Jellyfin" and install it, then enter your server address and login details. If your TV does not have a Jellyfin app, you can usually cast to it from the phone app, or use the TV's built-in web browser to access the Jellyfin web interface.

Step 9 — Keep Your USB Drive Always Mounted

By default, Raspberry Pi OS only mounts your USB drive after someone logs in at the desktop. If the Pi reboots overnight, Jellyfin may lose access to your files. To make the drive mount automatically at boot every time (even without anyone logged in), you need to add an entry to a file called /etc/fstab.

In plain words: /etc/fstab is a configuration file that tells Raspberry Pi OS exactly which drives to connect and where to put them every time it starts up. Think of it as a shopping list the Pi reads at boot time — "attach this drive, put it here".

First, find the unique ID of your USB drive. This ID is called a UUID — a long string of letters and numbers that never changes even if you plug the drive into a different USB port:

sudo blkid

blkid ("block ID") lists every storage device and shows its UUID and filesystem type. Find the line for your USB drive — match it by the label or size you noted earlier. Copy the UUID="..." value carefully. For FAT32/exFAT drives it looks something like A1B2-C3D4; for NTFS or ext4 drives it is a longer string like a1b2c3d4-e5f6-7890-abcd-ef1234567890.

In plain words: A UUID (Universally Unique Identifier) is a permanent serial number assigned to a storage drive when it is formatted. It never changes, so the Pi can identify your drive even if you plug it into a different USB socket.

Now create a permanent folder where the drive will always appear:

sudo mkdir -p /mnt/media

Creates the folder /mnt/media. The -p flag means "also create any missing parent folders and do not complain if the folder already exists". This folder is the permanent location — the mount point — where your USB drive's files will be accessible after we set up automatic mounting.

Next, give Jellyfin permission to read the drive's contents. Jellyfin runs as a background service under its own system user called jellyfin. The jellyfin user needs to be able to read the files on your USB drive. The easiest way is to add the jellyfin user to the same group that owns the mount point:

sudo usermod -aG pi jellyfin

usermod modifies a user account. -aG pi means "append (add without removing existing) the user to the group named pi". jellyfin is the user being modified. Adding the jellyfin service account to the pi group lets it read files owned by pi, which includes the default automatic mounts under /media/pi/. This change takes effect after Jellyfin is restarted in a moment.

Now open the fstab file to add your drive's entry:

sudo nano /etc/fstab

Opens the file /etc/fstab in the nano text editor with administrator permission. This is the file that controls automatic drive mounting. Be careful here — a mistake can prevent the Pi from booting. (If that happens, boot into recovery mode and edit the file to remove the incorrect line.)

Use the arrow keys to move to the very bottom of the file. Add one new line. Use the correct filesystem type for your drive:

For a FAT32 drive (FSTYPE was vfat):

UUID=YOUR-UUID-HERE  /mnt/media  vfat  defaults,uid=1000,gid=1000,umask=002,nofail,x-systemd.automount  0  0

Each part of this line explained: UUID=YOUR-UUID-HERE identifies the exact drive by its unique ID (paste the UUID you copied). /mnt/media is where to make the drive's contents available. vfat is the filesystem type (FAT32). defaults uses standard mount settings. uid=1000,gid=1000 makes files on the drive appear to be owned by the default pi user (user ID 1000 on Raspberry Pi OS). umask=002 makes files readable and writable by the owner and group, readable by everyone else. nofail means the Pi still boots normally even if the drive is absent. x-systemd.automount tells the system to wait until the drive is genuinely ready before mounting it (more reliable for USB drives that take a moment to spin up). The two 0s at the end disable filesystem checking at boot, which is recommended for USB drives.

For an exFAT drive (FSTYPE was exfat):

UUID=YOUR-UUID-HERE  /mnt/media  exfat  defaults,uid=1000,gid=1000,umask=002,nofail,x-systemd.automount  0  0

Identical to the FAT32 line above except exfat replaces vfat as the filesystem type. This requires the exfatprogs package installed in Step 2.

For an NTFS drive (FSTYPE was ntfs):

UUID=YOUR-UUID-HERE  /mnt/media  ntfs-3g  defaults,uid=1000,gid=1000,umask=002,nofail,x-systemd.automount  0  0

Identical to the other lines except ntfs-3g is the filesystem type. This uses the ntfs-3g driver installed in Step 2 to provide full read and write access. Note the filesystem type here is ntfs-3g (with a hyphen), not just ntfs.

For a Linux ext4 drive (FSTYPE was ext4):

UUID=YOUR-UUID-HERE  /mnt/media  ext4  defaults,nofail,x-systemd.automount  0  2

For ext4 Linux-formatted drives, no uid/gid/umask options are needed — Linux permissions on the drive itself control access. The last digit is 2 instead of 0, which allows the filesystem integrity check to run at boot (recommended for ext4). You may still need to adjust permissions on the drive's folders so the jellyfin user can read them (see the Tip below).

Save and exit nano: press Ctrl+O, then Enter to confirm the filename, then Ctrl+X to exit.

Test the new fstab entry without rebooting:

sudo mount -a

Tells the Pi to mount everything listed in /etc/fstab right now. If you see no error output, the line is correct and your drive is now accessible at /mnt/media. If you see an error message, open /etc/fstab again with sudo nano /etc/fstab, check the line carefully (UUID matches? filesystem type correct?), fix it, save, and run sudo mount -a again.

Restart Jellyfin so it picks up the group change and the new mount:

sudo systemctl restart jellyfin

Stops and restarts the Jellyfin service. This is needed so Jellyfin (running as the jellyfin user) picks up the group membership change made with usermod earlier, and also reconnects to the newly mounted drive path.

Now go back into the Jellyfin web interface at http://192.168.1.42:8096, log in, go to the menu icon (top-left) and choose Dashboard → Libraries. Edit your library and update the folder path to /mnt/media (or the subfolder within it where your films live). Jellyfin will re-scan and the library will work reliably after every reboot.

Tip: If Jellyfin still reports it cannot access the folder after all the above steps, check the folder permissions by running ls -la /mnt/media in the terminal. If the folder shows as owned by root with no group or other read access, run sudo chmod -R a+rX /mnt/media to make it readable by all users including the Jellyfin service.

4K and HEVC: What the Pi 5 Can Handle

In plain words: HEVC (also called H.265) is a modern video compression format used for most 4K films — it makes huge 4K video files a manageable size. Direct play means a client device plays the file in its original format with no conversion needed. Transcoding means the server converts the video from one format to another on the fly so a device can play it — this requires much more processing power.

The Pi 5's VideoCore VII graphics chip can hardware-decode HEVC (H.265) and H.264 video — the two most common formats for 4K and 1080p films. This means it can handle 4K direct play to a TV connected via HDMI with ease, barely breaking a sweat.

If a client device (such as an older phone or a browser on a laptop or desktop) cannot play HEVC natively, Jellyfin will transcode the video on the fly. The Pi 5 can handle a single 1080p software transcode comfortably. Transcoding a 4K stream in real time is much heavier work and may cause stuttering; if this happens, lower the quality setting in the Jellyfin player.

Tip: For the best experience, prefer client devices that support HEVC natively — most modern smartphones and smart TVs do. When HEVC is supported, Jellyfin uses direct play and the Pi barely works at all, leaving plenty of spare power for other tasks.

Enabling Hardware Transcoding in Jellyfin

If transcoding is necessary, you can enable hardware-accelerated transcoding to use the Pi 5's GPU rather than the main processor. This is optional but worthwhile if you regularly transcode:

  1. In the Jellyfin web interface, log in as admin and click the menu icon, then choose Dashboard.
  2. Under Server, click Playback.
  3. Find the Hardware acceleration dropdown and select Video4Linux2 (V4L2). This is the correct option for the Raspberry Pi 5's VideoCore VII GPU.
  4. Scroll down and click Save.
Note: Hardware transcoding on the Pi 5 via V4L2 works best with H.264 and HEVC content. For very unusual codecs (such as older AVI or WMV files), the Pi may fall back to software transcoding automatically.

Controlling Playback

HDMI-CEC: Use Your TV Remote

In plain words: HDMI-CEC (Consumer Electronics Control) is a hidden signalling channel built into the HDMI standard. It lets devices on your TV's HDMI ports talk to each other — for example, it lets your TV remote control your Pi, or lets the Pi automatically switch your TV on and select its input when the Pi starts playing something.

The Pi 5 supports HDMI-CEC, and Raspberry Pi OS enables it by default. If you are using Kodi (or another media player app) directly on the Pi with its HDMI output connected to your TV:

  • Your TV remote's arrow keys and OK button will navigate the on-screen menus.
  • The volume buttons on your TV remote will adjust the audio level.
  • Starting a media player can automatically wake the TV and switch it to the Pi's HDMI input.

CEC must also be enabled on your TV. Look in your TV's settings — brands use their own names for the same feature:

TV Brand Their name for HDMI-CEC Where to find it
Samsung Anynet+ Settings → General → External Device Manager → Anynet+
LG SimpLink Settings → General → SIMPLINK (HDMI-CEC)
Sony Bravia Sync Settings → Watching TV → External inputs → Bravia Sync
Philips EasyLink Setup → TV settings → EasyLink
Panasonic VIERA Link Setup → VIERA Link settings
Note: HDMI-CEC control works automatically for Kodi and LibreELEC once it is enabled on the TV. For Jellyfin (where you watch on a separate phone, tablet, or smart TV), your TV remote already controls that device natively — no extra setup is needed.

Phone Remote Apps

If you are using Kodi on your Pi, install the free Kodi Remote app (search for it in the App Store or Google Play Store) to control playback from your phone over Wi-Fi — pause, skip, browse your library, and adjust volume, all from the sofa.

For Jellyfin, the Jellyfin mobile app doubles as a remote control — tap the cast icon in the app to control playback on a TV or another device on your network.

Alternative: Installing Kodi on Raspberry Pi OS

If you would rather just have a local media player on your Pi without setting up a server, Kodi is a single command away. Kodi is the right choice if your films are on a USB drive plugged directly into the Pi and you only ever watch on the TV that the Pi is connected to.

sudo apt update
sudo apt install kodi -y

The first line refreshes the list of available software packages from the internet, so the Pi knows about the latest version of Kodi. The second line installs Kodi from Raspberry Pi OS's built-in software repository. The -y flag answers "yes" to the confirmation prompt automatically. After this completes (it may take a few minutes to download), you will find Kodi in the application menu under Sound & Video, or you can launch it by typing kodi in the terminal and pressing Enter.

Once Kodi opens, use the Videos → Files → Add videos... option from the home screen to point it at your USB drive. Kodi will scan the folder, download artwork, and build a library automatically — very similar to Jellyfin's setup wizard, but entirely local on the Pi.

Tip: If you want Kodi to launch automatically when the Pi boots (so it behaves like a dedicated set-top box), you can create a small autostart file. In the terminal, run: mkdir -p /home/pi/.config/autostart and then create a file at /home/pi/.config/autostart/kodi.desktop containing: 
[Desktop Entry]
Type=Application
Name=Kodi
Exec=kodi
This makes Kodi launch automatically when your Pi desktop starts up. For most beginners, though, simply launching Kodi from the application menu when you want to watch something is perfectly fine.
Careful: The autostart method above places a plain text file in your home folder. It is completely safe and easy to undo — simply delete the file (rm /home/pi/.config/autostart/kodi.desktop) and Kodi will stop auto-launching. It does not modify any system files.

Troubleshooting Common Problems

Problem Likely cause What to do
Cannot reach Jellyfin from another device The other device is on a different network, or you have the wrong IP address Make sure both devices are connected to the same Wi-Fi network (not one on 2.4 GHz and one on 5 GHz if your router keeps them separate). Re-run hostname -I on the Pi to confirm the current IP. Try accessing http://localhost:8096 in a browser on the Pi itself first — if that works, Jellyfin is fine and the issue is network connectivity.
Jellyfin cannot see my USB drive files The drive is not mounted, or Jellyfin does not have permission to read it Run lsblk to confirm the drive is mounted. Check the library path in Jellyfin's Dashboard → Libraries matches the actual mount point exactly (paths are case-sensitive). Check permissions with ls -la /mnt/media and ensure the jellyfin user can read the folder.
Video stutters or buffers Transcoding is happening and the Pi is under heavy load, or your Wi-Fi is slow In the Jellyfin player, lower the quality setting to Auto or 1080p. Connect the Pi to your router by Ethernet cable for maximum throughput. Enable hardware transcoding (V4L2) in Jellyfin's Dashboard → Playback settings. Prefer client devices that support HEVC natively so Jellyfin can use direct play.
No sound over HDMI The wrong audio output is selected (the Pi 5 has no 3.5 mm headphone jack — audio goes via HDMI or USB only) Right-click the speaker icon in the Pi taskbar and select the HDMI audio device. In Kodi, go to Settings → System → Audio and select the HDMI output device from the list.
TV remote does not control Kodi HDMI-CEC is disabled on the TV Find your TV brand's name for HDMI-CEC in the table above and enable it in your TV's settings. Then restart the Pi (or restart Kodi) for the Pi to detect the CEC connection.
Jellyfin library shows no artwork or wrong films Jellyfin could not match the files because the filenames do not include the film title and year Rename your files to include the film title and year in brackets — e.g. Dune (2021).mkv — then go to Dashboard → Libraries, select your library, and click Scan All Libraries to trigger a fresh scan.
Pi's IP address has changed (Jellyfin address stopped working) Your router assigned a different IP to the Pi after a reboot Re-run hostname -I to find the new address. To prevent this in future, log into your router's admin page and set a "DHCP reservation" for the Pi's MAC address so it always gets the same IP. See your router's manual for how to do this.
drive not mounting at boot despite fstab entry The UUID is wrong or the filesystem type is incorrect in fstab Run sudo blkid and compare the UUID with what is in /etc/fstab. Open fstab with sudo nano /etc/fstab, correct the UUID or filesystem type, save, then run sudo mount -a to test.

Keeping Jellyfin Updated

Because you installed Jellyfin via its official repository, updating it is the same as updating everything else on your Pi:

sudo apt update && sudo apt upgrade -y

This is the same update command you use for all software on your Pi. Because Jellyfin was added as a proper repository, this single command will also fetch and install any new version of Jellyfin automatically. Running this once a month is a good habit — you get bug fixes, security patches, and new features in one go.

Tip: After a Jellyfin update, the service restarts automatically. Check it is still running with sudo systemctl status jellyfin if you want to confirm.

Key terms

  • Kodi — a free, open-source media player application you install on top of Raspberry Pi OS; plays films, music, and TV shows stored on a local drive.
  • LibreELEC — a tiny operating system built entirely around Kodi; it replaces Raspberry Pi OS and is ideal for a dedicated media box.
  • Jellyfin — a free, open-source media server that runs on your Pi and streams your personal library to phones, tablets, TVs, and browsers over your home network.
  • Media server — software that stores your media on one computer (the Pi) and sends it to other devices over the network, like a private Netflix in your home.
  • Repository (repo) — an online library of trusted software packages that your Pi downloads and installs software from.
  • IP address — a unique number (like 192.168.1.42) that identifies a device on a network; your Pi's "house number" on your home Wi-Fi.
  • Port number — a number appended to an IP address (like :8096) that identifies a specific service on a device; like a flat number within a building.
  • HEVC / H.265 — a modern video compression format used for most 4K content; the Pi 5 can decode it in hardware, making 4K playback smooth.
  • Direct play — when a client device plays a video file in its original format without the server having to convert it; the most efficient and highest-quality mode.
  • Transcoding — when the server converts a video from one format to another on the fly so a device can play it; more demanding on the Pi's processor.
  • HDMI-CEC — a built-in feature of the HDMI standard that lets devices on HDMI ports talk to each other; allows a TV remote to control the Pi.
  • UUID — a permanent unique identification code assigned to a storage drive; used in /etc/fstab to reliably identify a drive regardless of which USB port it is plugged into.
  • /etc/fstab — a system configuration file that tells Raspberry Pi OS which drives to connect and where to put them every time it starts up.
  • Mount / mount point — "mounting" a drive means making its contents accessible at a folder path on the system; the folder it appears at is called the mount point.
  • exFAT — a filesystem format commonly used on larger USB drives sold ready-to-use; readable on Windows, macOS, and (with exfatprogs) Linux.
  • NTFS — the filesystem format Windows uses on internal hard drives; readable on Linux with the ntfs-3g driver.
  • V4L2 — Video4Linux2; the interface that lets Jellyfin use the Pi 5's GPU for hardware-accelerated video transcoding, reducing the load on the main processor.

Chapter summary

  • The Pi 5 makes an excellent media centre — choose LibreELEC for a dedicated box, Jellyfin for streaming to multiple devices around the house, or Kodi on Raspberry Pi OS for a straightforward local player.
  • Jellyfin is installed from its official repository using a single installer command and streams films, TV, and music to any browser, phone app, or smart TV app on your home network.
  • Windows-formatted USB drives (exFAT or NTFS) need the exfatprogs or ntfs-3g package installed on Bookworm before they mount reliably.
  • Adding a correctly formatted line to /etc/fstab with the drive's UUID and the nofail,x-systemd.automount options ensures the USB media drive mounts automatically at every boot, so Jellyfin always has access to your files.
  • The Jellyfin service account (jellyfin) needs to be in the pi group, or the mount point needs to be world-readable, before Jellyfin can access files on the USB drive.
  • The Pi 5 decodes 4K HEVC video in hardware (VideoCore VII), making high-quality direct playback smooth and efficient without any special configuration.
  • Hardware-accelerated transcoding can be enabled in Jellyfin's Dashboard → Playback by selecting V4L2, which offloads conversion work to the GPU.
  • HDMI-CEC lets your existing TV remote control Kodi on the Pi; enable it in your TV's settings (Anynet+, SimpLink, Bravia Sync, EasyLink, or VIERA Link depending on your brand).
  • Update Jellyfin with sudo apt update && sudo apt upgrade -y — the same command used for all software on your Pi.
  • Only use media software with content you legally own or have the right to play.
