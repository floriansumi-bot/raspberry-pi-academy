Somewhere in a cupboard sits a dusty LEGO MINDSTORMS NXT 2.0 set — the orange-and-grey brick that could only ever be as clever as its own little firmware allowed. In this chapter you will tear that ceiling off. You will salvage the NXT's motors and sensors, wire them to a Raspberry Pi 5 through a proper motor driver, and give the whole thing a brain that runs real Python and a pair of AI eyes from a Pi Camera. By the end you will have built an autonomous rover that sees an object, thinks about where it is, and acts by steering itself towards it. Along the way you will learn to write a class of your own, decode an unusual connector, drive a DC motor safely through an H-bridge, read a quadrature encoder, and bend a 5 V signal down to a Pi-safe 3.3 V.

What You'll Build & Learn

This is the Maker Lab finale, and the most ambitious build in the course. We are going to reanimate a LEGO NXT robot: keep its Technic frame, motors and sensors, but replace its old brain with your Pi 5 — which can run an on-device AI model that recognises objects in a camera image.

The finished rover rolls forward, and when its camera spots a target (a ball, a person) it turns to keep that target centred and drives towards it. That one sentence hides three whole disciplines, and you will learn the named fundamentals behind each.

  • gpiozero Motor and RotaryEncoder — two ready-made objects: one drives a motor through an H-bridge with speed control, the other counts the clicks of the wheel's built-in encoder.
  • Functions and a control loop — a while loop that reads the world, decides, and acts, over and over: the heartbeat of every robot.

Electronics fundamentals

  • The RJ12 6P6C connector and the NXT pinouts — the odd little plug LEGO used, why ordinary phone leads do not fit, and exactly which of the six coloured wires does what.
  • The DC motor plus quadrature encoder — an NXT motor is a plain 9 V DC motor with a clever optical sensor built in that counts how far the shaft has turned.
  • The H-bridge motor driver — the circuit that lets a tiny 3.3 V GPIO pin command a hungry 9 V motor in either direction, without the motor ever touching the Pi directly.
  • A separate motor battery and a common ground — why the motors get their own battery pack, and why that pack's negative terminal must still join the Pi's ground.

Meet the NXT 2.0 Parts You Can Reuse

Before we cut a single wire, let's be honest about what is easy to reuse on a Pi and what is genuinely hard. The NXT was a sealed system, so some parts give up their secrets readily and others fight back.

The NXT Connector, Decoded

Every NXT motor and sensor ends in the same plug: a six-position, six-conductor modular connector, written 6P6C. It looks almost exactly like the RJ12 connector on an old telephone handset cable — and that "almost" is the whole problem.

In plain words: 6P6C means "6 positions, 6 conductors" — a little clear plastic plug with six gold contacts in a row, held in by a springy plastic latch. RJ12 is the common name for the wiring standard that uses this exact plug.

LEGO played a sneaky trick: on an NXT plug the locking latch is offset to one side (the right) rather than centred. A standard phone or RJ12 lead has a centred latch, so it pushes into an NXT socket but will not seat or click home — the contacts do not line up reliably. This was deliberate, to stop people plugging the wrong things in.

That leaves you two routes to the wires:

  1. Buy NXT-compatible RJ12 sockets. A handful of breakout sockets with the correct right-offset latch are sold cheaply (search "NXT/EV3 RJ12 jack breakout"). You plug an unmodified LEGO cable in and screw-terminals or header pins come out. Tidy, reversible, and you keep your cables intact. This is the gentlest option.
  2. Cut a LEGO cable open — the salvage route. Take a spare NXT lead, snip it in the middle, and strip back the grey outer sheath to expose the six coloured inner wires. Now you have six tinned ends you can poke straight into a breadboard. You sacrifice one cable but need no special parts. This is the route we will assume, in the spirit of the e-waste pile.
Careful: Only ever cut a cable that is not plugged into anything, and never the cable to a sensor you cannot replace. Strip the outer sheath gently with wire strippers (or careful fingernails) so you do not nick the thin inner wires. Once exposed, the six wires follow a fixed colour code, which we map next.
Note: The colour of each inner wire is consistent across LEGO cables, but the wire colour does not follow the resistor/power conventions you have used elsewhere — here "red" is a ground wire and "green" is a power wire, which feels back-to-front. Always go by the table below, not by what a colour "usually" means.

Inside the NXT Motor

An NXT servo motor sounds fancy, but electrically it is two simple things in one housing: a plain 9 V DC motor with a built-in gearbox (strong but not fast), and a built-in optical quadrature encoder that reports how far the shaft has turned.

In plain words: A DC motor spins when you put a voltage across its two terminals; swap the two wires over and it spins the other way. There is no "position" built in — it just turns while powered. It is not a hobby servo (those expect a special pulse signal); it is much simpler than that.
In plain words: A quadrature encoder is a sensor that counts how far a shaft has rotated by watching a striped disc spin past two light sensors. "Quadrature" means the two sensors are offset by a quarter-step so that, by comparing which one changes first, you can tell both how far and which direction the shaft turned.

The NXT encoder gives roughly one count per degree — about 360 counts per full revolution of the output shaft. That is plenty to drive a precise distance or keep two wheels matched so the robot rolls straight.

The motor's six-wire pinout

Here is the exact mapping for a MOTOR cable. Pin 1 is on the left when you look into the plug with the latch facing away from you.

4GreenEncoder supply (+4.3 V)Pi 3.3 V (powers the encoder)

The two power wires (white/black) carry the heavy current that spins the motor — these never go near the Pi, only the driver. The other four belong to the encoder: a power pair (green/red) and the two signal channels (yellow/blue) we read.

Electrical specs you must respect

  • Motor voltage: about 9 V (a fresh 6×AA pack). It will also run more gently at 6 V.
  • No-load current: low — only a few tens of milliamps when spinning freely.
  • Stall current: high — up to roughly 1 A when the motor is jammed and cannot turn. This is the figure that matters when choosing a driver.
  • Encoder supply: the green wire wants about 4.3–5 V; the Pi's 3.3 V rail powers it well enough in practice for our purposes, and keeps the signal swing lower (a bonus for the divider).
Careful: Never connect the motor's white/black power wires to a GPIO pin or to the 3.3 V/5 V pins. A motor drawing up to ~1 A would instantly overload the Pi, and when a spinning motor is switched off it generates a sharp reverse voltage spike (we explain this next) that can destroy a GPIO pin. Power wires go to the H-bridge, full stop.

Driving the Motors Safely — the H-bridge

You already know the golden rule from Chapters 36 and 38: never drive a motor straight from a GPIO pin. It is doubly true for an NXT motor.

  • Current. A GPIO pin can safely source only about 16 mA. An NXT motor wants tens of milliamps to spin freely and up to ~1 A if it stalls — fifty times too much. The pin (and possibly the whole Pi) would burn out.
  • Inductive kickback. A motor is a coil of wire. Cut the current to a coil suddenly and it fights back with a sharp voltage spike of the opposite polarity. Fed into a 3.3 V GPIO pin, that spike is lethal.

The answer is an H-bridge: a small chip between the Pi and the motor. The Pi sends gentle low-current 3.3 V logic signals; the chip switches the motor's heavy current on its behalf and can reverse its direction.

In plain words: An H-bridge is four electronic switches arranged in an "H" shape around the motor. Close one diagonal pair and current flows one way (motor forwards); close the other pair and it flows the other way (motor backwards). The chip also contains protection diodes that safely swallow the inductive kickback so it never reaches the Pi.

Which driver to use

For a pair of NXT motors, a small dual H-bridge (two motors on one board) is ideal. Three good choices:

Driver Motor voltage Current per motor Verdict for NXT motors
DRV8833 (recommended) 2.7–10.8 V ~1.5 A continuous, 2 A peak Perfect. Handles a 9 V pack and the NXT's ~1 A stall with margin to spare. Small, cheap, efficient.
TB6612FNG 2.5–13.5 V ~1.2 A continuous, 3 A peak Also excellent and very efficient. A great alternative if you have one to hand.
L298N (the cheap classic) up to ~35 V ~2 A Works, but it is bulky, wastes ~2 V as heat, and needs a heatsink. Use only if it is what you already own.

We will use the DRV8833. Each of its two channels has two inputs (IN1, IN2) and two outputs (OUT1, OUT2). The Pi drives the inputs; the outputs go to one motor's white/black power pair. gpiozero's Motor(forward=IN1, backward=IN2) drives those inputs for you, pulsing them rapidly (PWM) to control speed.

In plain words: PWM (pulse-width modulation) means switching a pin on and off very fast. If it is on 30% of the time the motor behaves as though it is getting 30% of full power, so the wheel turns slowly. gpiozero does this automatically when you call motor.forward(0.3).

The separate-battery and common-ground rule

This is the most important wiring idea in the chapter, so read it twice.

  • Give the motors their own battery. The Pi's 5 V supply is for the Pi's brain; it cannot spare amps for motors, and motor noise can crash the Pi. Use a separate ~9 V pack (six AA cells is ideal) wired to the driver's motor-power input (often labelled VM or VMOT).
  • But join the grounds. The motor battery's negative terminal, the driver's GND and the Pi's GND must all connect to one shared ground. That shared "0 V" reference is what lets the Pi's 3.3 V logic signals mean anything to the driver. Without a common ground nothing works, and floating grounds behave unpredictably.
Careful — the safety rules for this build:
  • Power off and unplug the Pi before wiring anything. Only re-power after a full check.
  • Use a separate battery for the motors — never try to spin them from the Pi's 5 V pin.
  • Share a common ground between the motor battery, the driver, and the Pi.
  • Never feed motor voltage back into the Pi. The motor's 9 V power pair touches only the driver's outputs.
  • Level-shift the sensor and encoder lines down to 3.3 V (with a divider) before they reach any GPIO input — these lines can sit near 5 V.

Code: a Robot Class You Can Drive

Time for Python. We will start with one motor to prove the wiring, then wrap both motors in a Robot class so the rest of the program can say robot.forward() without caring about pins.

First, one motor

Open Thonny and type this short test. It assumes the left motor's inputs are on GPIO 20 and 21.

from gpiozero import Motor
from time import sleep

left = Motor(forward=20, backward=21)

left.forward(0.5)     # half speed forwards
sleep(2)
left.backward(0.5)    # half speed backwards
sleep(2)
left.stop()

Line 1 — from gpiozero import Motor: Imports the ready-made Motor object, which knows how to drive a two-input H-bridge channel.

Line 4 — left = Motor(forward=20, backward=21): Creates a motor object. forward=20 is the GPIO that drives AIN1; backward=21 drives AIN2. Naming the arguments stops us mixing the two up.

Line 6 — left.forward(0.5): Spins the motor forwards at half speed. The 0.5 is a fraction from 0 (stopped) to 1 (full speed); gpiozero turns it into PWM on the input pin for you.

Line 7 — sleep(2): Keeps it running for two seconds so you can watch.

Line 8 — left.backward(0.5): Reverses direction at half speed by driving the other input.

Line 10 — left.stop(): Switches both inputs off, letting the motor coast to a halt.

Note: If "forward" actually spins the wheel backwards, you have two easy fixes: swap the motor's white and black wires at the driver output, or swap forward= and backward= in the code. Either is fine — pick one.

Now the Robot class

A wheeled robot's two motors must do coordinated things: both forward, both backward, or one each way to spin on the spot. Rather than repeat motor commands everywhere, we bundle the pair into a Robot class with friendly methods. Save this as robot.py.

from gpiozero import Motor


class Robot:
    """Two NXT motors wired to a DRV8833, wrapped as one robot."""

    def __init__(self, left_pins, right_pins, speed=0.6):
        self.left = Motor(forward=left_pins[0], backward=left_pins[1])
        self.right = Motor(forward=right_pins[0], backward=right_pins[1])
        self.speed = speed

    def forward(self, speed=None):
        s = speed if speed is not None else self.speed
        self.left.forward(s)
        self.right.forward(s)

    def backward(self, speed=None):
        s = speed if speed is not None else self.speed
        self.left.backward(s)
        self.right.backward(s)

    def turn_left(self, speed=None):
        s = speed if speed is not None else self.speed
        self.left.backward(s)
        self.right.forward(s)

    def turn_right(self, speed=None):
        s = speed if speed is not None else self.speed
        self.left.forward(s)
        self.right.backward(s)

    def stop(self):
        self.left.stop()
        self.right.stop()


# A quick demo when you run this file directly
if __name__ == "__main__":
    from time import sleep
    robot = Robot(left_pins=(20, 21), right_pins=(19, 26))
    robot.forward(); sleep(1.5)
    robot.turn_right(); sleep(0.7)
    robot.forward(); sleep(1.5)
    robot.stop()

Line 1 — from gpiozero import Motor: We will build two Motor objects inside our class.

Line 4 — class Robot:: Starts our own blueprint. Anything indented underneath belongs to it. The triple-quoted line just below is a docstring describing the class.

Line 7 — def __init__(self, left_pins, right_pins, speed=0.6):: The initialiser, run automatically when you build a Robot. It expects the two pins for each side (as a pair like (20, 21)) and a default driving speed.

Lines 8–9 — self.left = Motor(...) / self.right = Motor(...): Build one Motor object per side and store them on this robot. left_pins[0] is the forward pin, left_pins[1] the backward pin.

Line 10 — self.speed = speed: Remembers the default speed so methods can fall back to it.

Line 12 — def forward(self, speed=None):: A method. speed=None means "if you don't pass a speed, use my default".

Line 13 — s = speed if speed is not None else self.speed: Picks the speed to use: the one passed in, or the stored default. A compact one-line if.

Lines 14–15 — drive both motors forward: Calls forward() on each side at the chosen speed, so the robot rolls straight ahead.

Lines 17–20 — backward: The mirror image — both motors reverse.

Lines 22–25 — turn_left: Spins on the spot to the left by running the left wheel backward and the right wheel forward.

Lines 27–30 — turn_right: The opposite — left forward, right backward.

Lines 32–34 — stop: Stops both motors. We will call this whenever the program ends so the robot never runs off.

Line 38 — if __name__ == "__main__":: Code under this line runs only when you launch robot.py directly, not when another file imports the class. It is a tidy way to ship a class with a built-in self-test.

Lines 40–45 — the demo: Build a robot with our pin choices, drive forward, turn, drive forward again, then stop. A short, safe shake-down run.

Run steps

  1. Lift the wheels off the ground. Rest the chassis on a mug or box so the wheels spin freely in the air. Never test-drive a new robot on the floor.
  2. Fit fresh batteries in the motor pack and switch it on. Power up the Pi separately.
  3. Open robot.py in Thonny and press the green Run button (F5).
  4. Watch the wheels: both should spin forward, then the right side should reverse briefly for the turn, then both forward again, then stop. If a wheel goes the wrong way, swap that motor's white/black wires.
  5. Press the red Stop button in Thonny if anything misbehaves — it cuts the program and the motors.

Reading the Wheels — the Encoder

Driving blind is fine for a demo, but a real robot wants to know how far it has gone. That is the job of the motor's built-in quadrature encoder, read via its two channels (the yellow and blue wires) with gpiozero's RotaryEncoder object.

Wiring the encoder — through a divider

The encoder's two signal wires can swing up towards 4–5 V, which is too high for a 3.3 V GPIO input. So each one goes through the same kind of voltage divider you built in Chapter 38 before reaching the Pi.

  • Green (encoder +supply) → Pi 3.3 V pin. Powering the encoder from 3.3 V keeps its output swing lower, which is kinder to the divider.
  • Red (encoder GND) → common ground.
  • Yellow (channel A) → through a 330 Ω / 470 Ω divider → GPIO input.
  • Blue (channel B) → through a second 330 Ω / 470 Ω divider → GPIO input.
Careful: Even powered at 3.3 V the encoder output can briefly exceed the Pi's safe limit, so keep the dividers in place. Reusing the proven 330 Ω (R1, signal-to-tap) and 470 Ω (R2, tap-to-GND) pair drops 5 V to about 2.94 V — safely readable as a HIGH. The GPIO pin connects to the tap between the two resistors.

Here is a short program that counts one wheel and prints how far it has turned. Save it as encoder_test.py.

from gpiozero import RotaryEncoder
from time import sleep

# Channel A on GPIO 16, channel B on GPIO 12 (both via dividers)
enc = RotaryEncoder(a=16, b=12, max_steps=0)

print("Turn the wheel by hand. Ctrl+C to stop.")
try:
    while True:
        steps = enc.steps
        degrees = steps          # ~1 count per degree on an NXT motor
        print(f"steps={steps:6d}   ~{degrees}°")
        sleep(0.3)
except KeyboardInterrupt:
    print("\nDone.")

Line 1 — from gpiozero import RotaryEncoder: Imports the object that watches two pins and keeps a running count of how far, and which way, the shaft has turned.

Line 5 — enc = RotaryEncoder(a=16, b=12, max_steps=0): Builds the encoder object. a and b are the two GPIO pins reading channels A and B (each via its divider). max_steps=0 tells gpiozero not to wrap or clamp the count, so it can grow as large as we like.

Line 10 — steps = enc.steps: Reads the steps attribute — the live count. It rises as the wheel turns one way and falls as it turns the other. That sign is the "quadrature" magic: the object compares which channel changed first to know the direction.

Line 11 — degrees = steps: Because an NXT encoder gives roughly one count per degree, the step count is already an approximate angle. For precise distance you would calibrate this against your wheel size.

Line 12 — print(...): Shows the count and approximate angle. ° is just the degree symbol (°).

Lines 14–15 — except KeyboardInterrupt: Lets Ctrl+C end the loop cleanly.

Driving a set distance with when_rotated

Instead of polling in a loop, the encoder can call a function of yours each time it moves, using its when_rotated event. Here is the idea — drive forward until the left wheel has turned a target number of steps, then stop.

from gpiozero import RotaryEncoder
from robot import Robot
from signal import pause

robot = Robot(left_pins=(20, 21), right_pins=(19, 26))
left_enc = RotaryEncoder(a=16, b=12, max_steps=0)

TARGET = 720          # ~two full wheel turns (720 ° at ~1 °/count)

def check_progress():
    if abs(left_enc.steps) >= TARGET:
        robot.stop()
        print("Reached target distance.")

left_enc.when_rotated = check_progress

robot.forward(0.5)
print("Driving... will stop after the target.")
pause()

Line 2 — from robot import Robot: Reuses the Robot class you wrote earlier by importing it from robot.py. This is why classes pay off — one tidy object, used everywhere.

Line 6 — left_enc = RotaryEncoder(a=16, b=12, max_steps=0): The encoder for the left wheel, on the same two divider-protected pins as before.

Line 8 — TARGET = 720: Our goal in encoder counts. At roughly one count per degree, 720 is about two full turns of the wheel — adjust to suit your wheel diameter.

Lines 10–13 — check_progress(): A function that asks "have we gone far enough?". abs(...) ignores direction so it works whichever way the count runs. When the target is reached it stops the robot.

Line 15 — left_enc.when_rotated = check_progress: Hands our function to the encoder as a callback. From now on, every time the wheel moves, gpiozero calls check_progress for us — no busy loop needed.

Line 17 — robot.forward(0.5): Starts both wheels rolling at half speed.

Line 19 — pause(): Keeps the program alive so the callback can keep firing until the target is hit.

Tip: To drive straight, read both wheels' encoders and nudge the faster wheel's speed down a little until the two counts match. Real robots wobble because no two motors are identical; the encoder is how you correct for it.

Reading a Sensor — the Touch Bumper

The touch sensor is the easiest part to reuse: under the plastic it is just a push switch — press it and two wires connect, release and they part. That is exactly what gpiozero's Button expects.

Find the two wires that close — the robust method

Rather than trust a fixed pinout (touch-sensor wiring varies, and a salvaged cable can be wired either way round), find the switch contacts yourself with a multimeter. This always works.

  1. Set your multimeter to continuity (the mode with the little speaker/diode symbol that beeps when two probes are connected).
  2. Expose the six wires of the touch sensor's cable (cut-and-strip, as before).
  3. Touch the probes to pairs of wires and press the sensor button. The pair that beeps only while pressed is your switch. On most NXT touch sensors these are the two signal/ground wires, but verify — do not assume.
  4. Mark those two wires. One becomes "signal", the other "ground".

Wire the "signal" wire to a GPIO pin and the "ground" wire to a Pi GND pin. gpiozero's Button switches on an internal pull-up or pull-down so you need no extra resistor. Save this as bumper.py.

from gpiozero import Button
from robot import Robot
from signal import pause

robot = Robot(left_pins=(20, 21), right_pins=(19, 26))
bumper = Button(17)            # the touch sensor's switch wires: GPIO 17 + GND

def on_bump():
    robot.stop()
    print("Bump! Stopped.")

bumper.when_pressed = on_bump

robot.forward(0.4)
print("Rolling forward until the bumper is hit. Ctrl+C to quit.")
pause()

Line 1 — from gpiozero import Button: Brings in the Button object, perfect for any simple on/off switch — including a salvaged NXT touch sensor.

Line 6 — bumper = Button(17): Creates the button on GPIO 17. The sensor's other switch wire goes to a GND pin. No external resistor is needed — gpiozero handles the pull internally.

Lines 8–10 — on_bump(): What to do when the bumper is hit: stop the robot and say so.

Line 12 — bumper.when_pressed = on_bump: Registers our function as the press callback, so a bump instantly halts the wheels.

Line 14 — robot.forward(0.4): Rolls gently forward; the robot will keep going until something presses the bumper.

Line 16 — pause(): Keeps the program running so the callback stays armed.

The ultrasonic sensor (advanced / optional): The NXT ultrasonic sensor is not a simple switch — it is an I²C device. I²C is a two-wire bus (a clock line and a data line, the yellow and blue wires here) that lets a chip and the Pi exchange numbers. You enable I²C in raspi-config, level-shift the two lines to 3.3 V, and read the sensor's distance register over the bus. Be honest with yourself: the NXT ultrasonic has fussy timing and was designed for LEGO's own brick, so it is a fiddly afternoon project, not a five-minute one. If you just want reliable distance, the HC-SR04 from Chapter 38 is far easier — reuse that instead.

Giving the Robot AI Eyes

Now the exciting part. In Chapter 19 you ran an AI model on your Pi; here we point it at a camera and let it steer the robot. The plan is the classic robotics loop, see → think → act:

  1. See — grab a frame from the Pi Camera with Picamera2 (the modern camera library on Bookworm).
  2. Think — run the frame through an on-device object-detection model that returns boxes around things it recognises.
  3. Act — find the target box's horizontal position and turn the Robot so the target moves towards the centre, then drive forward.
In plain words: Object detection is an AI task where a model looks at an image and draws a box around each thing it finds, with a label ("person", "ball", "cup") and a confidence score. We only care about where our chosen target's box is, left-to-right.

Two ways to run the model

  • TensorFlow Lite on the Pi 5 — a small model (MobileNet-SSD) runs on the Pi's own CPU. It needs no special hardware beyond a Camera Module 3, but on a Pi 5 you should honestly expect only a handful of frames per second for detection. That is plenty for a slow follower robot.
  • The Raspberry Pi AI Camera (Sony IMX500) — this clever camera runs the neural network inside the camera module itself and hands the Pi the results directly, so the Pi's CPU stays free and the frame rate is much higher. If you have one, it is the smoother choice. Either way the Python pattern is the same.

The follower program

This program follows the first person it sees (change the label to follow anything in the model's vocabulary). It keeps the AI part simple so the steering logic is clear. Save it as follower.py.

from picamera2 import Picamera2
from robot import Robot
import numpy as np
# 'detector' wraps your chosen model. See ch19 for loading a TFLite model.
from detector import detect_objects   # returns a list of {label, score, cx}

robot = Robot(left_pins=(20, 21), right_pins=(19, 26))

picam = Picamera2()
picam.configure(picam.create_preview_configuration(
    main={"size": (320, 240)}))
picam.start()

TARGET_LABEL = "person"
DEAD_ZONE = 40          # pixels either side of centre we call "centred"
CENTRE_X = 320 // 2     # the middle column of a 320-wide frame

print("Following. Ctrl+C to stop.")
try:
    while True:
        frame = picam.capture_array()
        objects = detect_objects(frame)

        targets = [o for o in objects
                   if o["label"] == TARGET_LABEL and o["score"] > 0.5]

        if not targets:
            robot.stop()                 # nothing to follow — wait
            continue

        target = max(targets, key=lambda o: o["score"])
        offset = target["cx"] - CENTRE_X

        if offset < -DEAD_ZONE:
            robot.turn_left(0.4)         # target is to the left
        elif offset > DEAD_ZONE:
            robot.turn_right(0.4)        # target is to the right
        else:
            robot.forward(0.4)           # centred — approach it

except KeyboardInterrupt:
    robot.stop()
    print("\nStopped.")

Line 1 — from picamera2 import Picamera2: The modern camera library on Raspberry Pi OS Bookworm. It gives us frames as plain image arrays.

Line 5 — from detector import detect_objects: We hide the AI details behind one helper, detect_objects(frame), which returns a list of detections — each a small dictionary with a label, a confidence score, and cx, the horizontal centre of the box in pixels. Building that helper from a TFLite model is exactly the Chapter 19 material, so we reuse it here rather than repeat it.

Lines 9–12 — set up the camera: Create a Picamera2, configure a small 320×240 preview (small frames are faster to detect), and start it.

Line 14 — TARGET_LABEL = "person": The thing we want to follow. Swap for any label the model knows.

Line 15 — DEAD_ZONE = 40: A tolerance band around the centre. If the target is within 40 pixels of the middle we treat it as "centred" and drive straight, rather than twitching left/right forever.

Line 16 — CENTRE_X = 320 // 2: The middle column of the frame — the position we want the target to sit at.

Line 21 — frame = picam.capture_array(): "SEE": grab the latest camera image as a numbers array.

Line 22 — objects = detect_objects(frame): "THINK": run the model and get back every object it found.

Lines 24–25 — filter: Keep only detections matching our target label and confident enough (score > 0.5), discarding the rest.

Lines 27–29 — nothing found: If no target is visible, stop and loop again. A follower that charges off when it loses sight of its target is a runaway robot.

Line 31 — target = max(targets, key=lambda o: o["score"]): If several match, pick the most confident one.

Line 32 — offset = target["cx"] - CENTRE_X: How far left (negative) or right (positive) of centre the target is. This single number is the whole "decision".

Lines 34–39 — "ACT": If the target is well to the left, turn left; well to the right, turn right; otherwise it is roughly centred, so drive forward to approach. Steering to reduce offset towards zero is what keeps the target in front of us.

Lines 41–43 — clean stop: Ctrl+C stops the motors so the robot never keeps rolling after the program ends.

Note: Be realistic about speed. A TFLite detection on a Pi 5 might take a couple of hundred milliseconds, so the robot reacts a few times a second, not instantly. Keep the drive speed low (0.3–0.4) so it does not overshoot between decisions. The IMX500 AI Camera, doing the maths on-sensor, reacts much faster.

Putting It All Together

You now hold every piece of an autonomous object-following rover:

  • A chassis built from the NXT's Technic frame and two drive motors.
  • A DRV8833 H-bridge with its own ~9 V battery and a common ground to the Pi.
  • A Robot class giving clean forward/turn/stop control.
  • An encoder on each wheel for distance and going straight.
  • A touch bumper as a safety stop.
  • A camera + AI model closing the see → think → act loop.

To combine them, run the follower loop as the main program, but keep the bumper's when_pressed callback armed so that a physical bump always wins and stops the robot, no matter what the camera thinks. A good robot has a reflex that overrides its brain.

A safe start (arm / disarm)

Never let a vision robot leap into motion the instant the program starts — the camera might already see its target and bolt. Add a simple "armed" guard: the robot only drives once you have pressed the bumper once to say "I'm ready", and the same bumper stops it again.

armed = False

def toggle_arm():
    global armed
    armed = not armed
    if not armed:
        robot.stop()
    print("ARMED" if armed else "DISARMED")

bumper.when_pressed = toggle_arm

# inside the main loop, only move when armed:
#   if armed and targets:
#       ... steer ...
#   else:
#       robot.stop()

Line 1 — armed = False: A flag that starts off, so the robot is still until you say otherwise.

Lines 3–8 — toggle_arm(): Flips the flag each press. global armed lets the function change the outer variable. When disarming, it stops the motors immediately.

Line 10 — bumper.when_pressed = toggle_arm: One press arms, the next disarms — a simple, physical on/off you can reach without the keyboard.

Lines 12–16 — the guard (shown as a comment): In your main loop, only steer when armed is true; otherwise hold still. This is the difference between a controllable robot and one that surprises you.

The Easy Route: BrickPi3

Sidebar — don't fancy soldering or cutting cables? The BrickPi3 (originally Dexter Industries, now Modular Robotics) is a HAT that sits on top of your Pi and provides four proper NXT/EV3 sockets — for both motors and sensors — with the correct right-offset latch. You plug your unmodified LEGO cables straight in and control everything from a Python library, with the H-bridges and level-shifting already done on the board.
  • Pi 5 support: the BrickPi3 does work with a Pi 5, but with caveats — the Pi 5 draws more power than the BrickPi3 can comfortably supply, so power the Pi separately, and the acrylic case/fan clearance is tight. Check the current BrickPi3 notes before you buy.
  • When to choose it: if you want to reuse NXT/EV3 parts with the least fuss, or you are teaching a class and cannot have students cutting cables, the BrickPi3 is the gentle path. You lose the deep understanding of H-bridges and dividers that the DIY route teaches — which is precisely why this chapter builds the DIY version first.
Careful: The official Raspberry Pi Build HAT is not a substitute here. It uses LEGO's newer LPF2/SPIKE connector, not the NXT's RJ12, so NXT motors and sensors will not plug into it. For NXT parts, it is DIY-with-an-H-bridge or a BrickPi3.

Troubleshooting

SymptomLikely causeFix
Motors do nothing at all No common ground, or driver in standby Join the motor battery −, driver GND and Pi GND. Tie any SLP/STBY pin to 3.3 V. Check the battery is switched on.
One wheel spins backwards That motor's power pair is reversed Swap its white and black wires at the driver output, or swap forward=/backward= in code.
Pi reboots when motors start Motors drawing from the Pi, or grounds bouncing Confirm motor power comes from the separate battery's VM, not the Pi's 5 V. Use fresh cells. Keep motor wires short.
Encoder count never changes Wrong wires, no encoder power, or divider error Green→3.3 V, Red→GND, Yellow/Blue→GPIO via dividers. Confirm the GPIO taps to the join between R1 and R2.
Encoder count jumps erratically Floating input or no divider Make sure both channels go through their dividers and that the encoder shares the common ground.
Bumper never triggers Wrong two wires chosen Re-do the multimeter continuity test; use the pair that beeps only while pressed.
Camera follower never moves Model not loaded, or label not in its vocabulary Test detect_objects on its own first (print the labels it returns). Pick a label the model actually knows.
Robot overshoots / weaves Drive speed too high for the frame rate Lower the speed to 0.3, widen the DEAD_ZONE, or use the faster IMX500 AI Camera.
No module named 'lgpio' The lgpio backend is missing Run sudo apt install python3-lgpio, then restart Thonny.

Safety Checklist

Before every powered test, run down this list. It takes ten seconds and saves Pis.

  1. Separate motor battery. Motors are powered by their own ~9 V pack at the driver's VM — never from the Pi.
  2. Common ground. Battery −, driver GND and Pi GND all meet at one shared 0 V rail.
  3. Level-shifted signals. Every NXT sensor/encoder line that can exceed 3.3 V passes through a divider before reaching a GPIO pin.
  4. Motor isolation. The motors' white/black power wires touch only the driver outputs — never a GPIO or power pin.
  5. Current within limits. The driver is rated above the motor's ~1 A stall current (the DRV8833 is, comfortably).
  6. Wheels off the ground first. Test new code with the chassis propped up so a bug cannot send it off a table.
  7. Power off to rewire. Shut down and unplug the Pi before changing any wiring; re-power only after a full check.

Make It Your Own / Where to Go Next

You have built a genuinely intelligent robot from a box of "obsolete" LEGO. Here is where to take it:

  • Line following. Point a downward camera (or a swapped-in Pi-friendly light sensor) at the floor and steer to keep a dark line centred — the same offset-to-steering idea, no AI model needed.
  • Obstacle avoidance. Add the NXT ultrasonic over I²C (or an HC-SR04 from Chapter 38) and have the robot back up and turn when something gets too close.
  • Voice control. Combine with the speech work from Chapter 19 so you can say "follow me" or "stop" and have the robot obey.
  • A web joystick. Wrap the Robot class in a small Flask app (Chapter 41) so you can drive it from your phone's browser — manual override for when the AI gets confused.
  • Go EV3. The newer EV3 motors and sensors use the same RJ12 plug and similar ideas; a BrickPi3 or careful DIY wiring reuses those too.
Tip: Keep your Robot class as the foundation for all of these. Because the messy hardware lives inside one tidy object, every new project — line follower, web-driven car, voice rover — just imports Robot and gives it new orders. That is the real lesson of classes: write the hard part once, reuse it forever.

Key terms

  • 6P6C / RJ12 - the six-contact modular plug LEGO used; the NXT version has a right-offset latch so ordinary phone leads don't seat.
  • Quadrature encoder - a two-channel sensor that reports how far and which way a shaft turned; the NXT motor's gives ~1 count per degree.
  • H-bridge - a driver chip that switches a motor's heavy current and reverses its direction from gentle 3.3 V logic, absorbing inductive kickback.
  • DRV8833 - a recommended dual H-bridge (2.7–10.8 V, ~1.5 A/channel) ideal for two NXT motors.
  • PWM - rapid on/off switching that controls motor speed; gpiozero applies it when you pass a speed like 0.4.
  • Separate battery + common ground - motors get their own ~9 V supply, but its negative must join the Pi's ground as a shared 0 V reference.
  • Voltage divider / level-shift - two resistors dropping a >3.3 V signal (sensor or encoder line) to a Pi-safe level before a GPIO input.
  • Motor / RotaryEncoder - the gpiozero objects that drive an H-bridge channel and count an encoder (.steps, when_rotated).
  • Robot class - your own class wrapping both motors with forward/backward/turn_left/turn_right/stop.
  • Object detection - an AI task returning labelled boxes around things in an image; we use the box's horizontal centre to steer.
  • See → think → act - the robotics loop: capture a frame, run the model, decide, drive the motors, repeat.
  • BrickPi3 - a HAT with proper NXT/EV3 sockets that does the H-bridge and level-shifting for you (works on a Pi 5 with power caveats).

Chapter summary

  • An NXT motor is a plain 9 V DC motor with a built-in quadrature encoder (~1 count/degree); its six RJ12 wires split into a power pair (white/black) and a four-wire encoder (red, green, yellow, blue).
  • The NXT's 6P6C plug has a right-offset latch, so you either use an NXT-compatible breakout socket or cut a spare cable to reach the six colours.
  • Motors must be driven through an H-bridge (we used the DRV8833), never a GPIO pin, because of their current draw and inductive kickback.
  • Separate ~9 V battery for the motors, but a common ground with the Pi — the single most important wiring rule here.
  • The encoder's and sensors' lines can exceed 3.3 V, so they reach the Pi through a voltage divider (level-shifting).
  • You wrote a Robot class wrapping two gpiozero Motor objects, read wheels with RotaryEncoder, and read a touch bumper as a Button.
  • The AI layer follows the see → think → act loop: Picamera2 frame → object-detection model → steer the Robot towards the target. Expect a few frames per second on a Pi 5 with TFLite, faster with the IMX500 AI Camera.
  • The BrickPi3 is the easy off-the-shelf alternative; the Pi Build HAT is not, as it uses the newer LPF2 connector.
