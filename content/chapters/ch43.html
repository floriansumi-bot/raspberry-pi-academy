There is a dead smartphone in a drawer in almost every home — a cracked-screen Galaxy, a swollen-battery iPhone, a hand-me-down that simply stopped charging. The internet is full of breathless promises that you can rip out its camera, its screen, even its "graphics card", and plug them straight into a Raspberry Pi. The honest truth is that most of a modern phone cannot be reused that way at all — and this chapter will explain plainly why. But a handful of parts genuinely can be given a second life, and there is one brilliant trick that turns the whole phone into a wireless camera for your Pi. Along the way you will name and practise some real Python (functions, loops, and using external libraries like gpiozero and OpenCV) and real electronics (lithium-ion safety, a transistor motor driver, a class-D audio amp, and a boost converter) — with safety, always, coming first.

The Honest Truth About Phone Parts

Before we salvage a single screw, let us set expectations honestly. A modern smartphone is one of the most tightly integrated pieces of electronics ever made for the home. Tiny, proprietary, glued, and soldered — it is built to be a sealed unit, not a parts bin. That is exactly the opposite of the friendly, modular world of breadboards and jumper wires you met earlier in this Part.

In plain words: integrated means many separate jobs have been squeezed onto one chip or one tiny board. The more integrated a device is, the harder it is to pull one job back out and reuse it on its own — there are no neat, documented connectors, and no spare drivers to make it work elsewhere.

So the realistic picture is: a few parts are reusable, most are not, and being clear about which is which will save you hours of frustration (and protect your wallet and your Pi). Here is the honest map.

Name the Fundamentals

As always in the Maker Lab, we name out loud exactly what you are learning so that the skills stick and carry into your next project. This chapter is a little different from a single build — it is four mini-builds — so it touches several fundamentals, each taught with a small example before it is used.

Two of these — functions and loops — you will use everywhere. Let us warm up with a tiny isolated example before any hardware, so the camera code later feels familiar.

def buzz_pattern(times):
    for i in range(times):
        print(f"buzz {i + 1} of {times}")

buzz_pattern(3)

Line 1 — def buzz_pattern(times):: defines a function named buzz_pattern that takes one input (a parameter) called times. The indented lines below are its body and only run when the function is called.

Line 2 — for i in range(times):: a loop. range(times) produces the numbers 0, 1, 2… up to (but not including) times, and the body runs once for each, with i holding the current number.

Line 3 — print(f"buzz {i + 1} of {times}"): an f-string prints a tidy message; we add 1 to i so it counts from 1 for a human reader.

Line 5 — buzz_pattern(3): calls the function with the value 3, so the loop runs three times. Wrapping repeated steps in a function like this is exactly how we will drive the vibration motor in buzz patterns later.

Safety First: the Lithium-Ion Talk

Of everything in a phone, the lithium-ion battery is both the most tempting reuse and by far the most dangerous part. This section is firm on purpose. Read it fully before you so much as pick a cell up. The same iron rule you met in the e-waste lesson applies here, only closer up: never deform a lithium-ion cell.

In plain words: a lithium-ion cell stores a lot of energy in a very small space, separated by a microscopically thin membrane. Damage that membrane — by a puncture, a bend, a crush, or a short circuit — and the two sides touch internally. That causes a runaway chemical reaction called thermal runaway: the cell heats itself, vents flammable gas, and can catch fire or explode. Swelling (the cell puffing up with gas) is often the warning sign that comes first.
Careful — never use a damaged cell. If a phone battery is swollen, bulging, puffy, hot, hissing, leaking, or deformed in any way, do not use it, do not charge it, and never try to "deflate" it by pressing or piercing it. Put it somewhere non-flammable and take it to a battery recycling point. A swollen cell is a fire that has not happened yet.
Careful — never puncture, bend, crush or short a cell. Do not cut into it, drop it, drill it, or let its two terminals touch a piece of metal (or each other). Any of these can trigger thermal runaway in seconds. Treat a bare cell with the same respect you would a small petrol container.
Careful — never remove the protection PCB, and always charge attended. Many phone cells have a small protection circuit board (PCM) on their tab that stops dangerous over-charge, over-discharge and short circuit. Never peel it off. When charging a salvaged cell, do it attended, on a non-flammable surface (a ceramic tile or metal tin, never on a sofa or carpet), and never overnight or unattended.
Tip — the responsible default: for most learners, the genuinely sensible choice is not a bare harvested cell at all, but a purpose-built Raspberry Pi UPS HAT or an 18650 power-bank UPS module. These are designed and certified for the job, include proper protection, and are matched to the Pi's power needs. Reuse the harvested cell only if it is in perfect condition and you understand every warning above. There is no shame in choosing the safe, ready-made option — it is the grown-up move.

Reuse 1 — the Battery as Backup Power

The dream is simple: power your Pi from a salvaged phone cell, or at least keep it running for a few minutes when the mains drops out. It can be done, but only through the correct chain of parts — and with an honest understanding of its limits.

A single phone cell sits between about 3.0 V (empty) and 4.2 V (full). The Pi wants a steady 5 V. So we need two helper boards between the cell and the Pi.

The two boards you need

  • A TP4056 charging module with protection (the variant with a DW01 + FS8205 protection chip, usually with a USB-C input). It safely charges the cell to 4.2 V using a constant-current then constant-voltage profile, and — crucially — cuts the load off if the cell is over-discharged or shorted. A plain TP4056 with no protection chip must never be used on a bare cell.
  • A 5 V boost converter (a "step-up" converter). The cell's 3.0–4.2 V is below 5 V, so we cannot simply use it; a boost converter lifts the lower voltage up to a steady 5 V output.
In plain words: a boost converter is a small board that takes a lower input voltage and raises it to a higher, steady output voltage. Think of it as a tiny pump that lifts the pressure up to where the Pi needs it. (Its sibling, a buck converter, lowers voltage instead.)

Careful: set the boost converter's output to exactly 5.0 V using a multimeter on its output terminals before you ever connect it to the Pi. Many boost boards ship set higher; feeding more than 5 V into the Pi can damage it. Also confirm common ground across the cell, charger, boost and Pi — without a shared ground the circuit will behave erratically or not at all. And as always: charge attended, on a non-flammable surface.
Careful — the honest Pi-5 caveat. The Raspberry Pi 5 wants up to 5 V at around 5 A (25 W) at its busiest, and if it does not detect a proper 5 V/5 A supply it limits its own USB ports to 600 mA. A single salvaged phone cell (only a few watt-hours) feeding a cheap boost converter simply cannot deliver that peak current — under load the voltage sags and the Pi browns out or reboots. This makes a harvested cell suitable only for light, headless use (a sensor node with nothing plugged in) or a short backup for a graceful shutdown. For anything more, use a purpose-built Pi 5 UPS HAT matched to your load. That is the responsible recommendation.

Notice there is no Python code in this reuse: it is pure power wiring, with no GPIO involved. The cell does not talk to your code — it just keeps the lights on. The next two reuses, by contrast, are real GPIO builds with code.

Reuse 2 — the Vibration Motor (Haptics)

Inside the phone is a tiny vibration motor — the part that makes your phone buzz silently in your pocket. Salvaging it is easy and safe, and it is a lovely way to add haptics (touch feedback) to a Pi project: a buzz when a sensor trips, a pattern to signal a notification, a pulse when a button is pressed.

In plain words: a phone's vibration motor is a small DC motor with an off-centre weight on its shaft (an ERM, eccentric rotating mass) or a flat coin-shaped version. When it spins, the lopsided weight throws the whole motor side to side — that wobble is the buzz you feel.

It is still a motor, and that means the same firm rule you met when driving a speaker applies here: never connect a motor straight to a GPIO pin. A motor pulls far more current than a pin can safely give, and worse, when its coil switches off it kicks back a sharp voltage spike that can destroy the pin. We protect the Pi with three cheap parts.

  • An NPN transistor (a 2N2222/PN2222A or a BC548) acting as a low-side switch — the tiny GPIO signal controls a much bigger current.
  • A base resistor (about 470 Ω, anything from 330 Ω to 1 kΩ is fine) between the GPIO pin and the transistor's base, to limit the small control current.
  • A flyback diode (a 1N4001) across the motor — mandatory — to soak up that switch-off voltage spike.
In plain words: a transistor is an electronic switch with no moving parts: a small current into its base lets a much larger current flow through it. A flyback diode is a one-way valve placed across a motor (or any coil) that gives the switch-off voltage spike a safe loop to die away in, instead of blasting back into your circuit.

Get the diode the right way round — its silver band must point toward the 5 V side; backwards it shorts your supply. And check the transistor's leg order against its datasheet: the 2N2222 and BC548 number their emitter, base and collector legs differently, and a swapped leg is the commonest reason the motor stays still.

The code: buzz patterns

From the Pi's point of view, this is just one GPIO line going on and off — exactly like blinking an LED. The transistor and 5 V supply quietly turn that into a buzzing motor. We will use gpiozero's DigitalOutputDevice for simple on/off buzzes, then upgrade to PWMOutputDevice for adjustable intensity.

from gpiozero import DigitalOutputDevice
from time import sleep

motor = DigitalOutputDevice(17)

def buzz(times, length=0.2, gap=0.15):
    for i in range(times):
        motor.on()
        sleep(length)
        motor.off()
        sleep(gap)

buzz(3)          # three short buzzes
sleep(0.6)
buzz(1, length=0.8)   # one long buzz

Line 1 — from gpiozero import DigitalOutputDevice: imports a simple on/off output object from the preinstalled gpiozero library. Unlike LED, the name DigitalOutputDevice makes clear this is a general output, here switching a transistor.

Line 2 — from time import sleep: brings in sleep(), which pauses the program for a given number of seconds — how we set the length of each buzz.

Line 4 — motor = DigitalOutputDevice(17): creates the output object on GPIO 17 (physical pin 11). Setting it HIGH sends the small base current that switches the transistor — and the motor — on.

Line 6 — def buzz(times, length=0.2, gap=0.15):: defines a function for a buzz pattern. times is how many buzzes; length and gap have default values, so you can call buzz(3) and still get sensible timing, or override them.

Line 7 — for i in range(times):: a loop that repeats the buzz the requested number of times.

Lines 8–11 — motor.on() / sleep(length) / motor.off() / sleep(gap): turn the motor on, hold it for length seconds, turn it off, then pause for gap seconds before the next buzz.

Lines 13–15 — the calls: buzz(3) gives three short buzzes; after a pause, buzz(1, length=0.8) gives one long one — by passing a different length we reuse the same function for a new pattern. That is the payoff of writing a function.

To control the strength of the buzz rather than just on/off, swap to PWM. PWM (pulse-width modulation) switches the pin on and off thousands of times a second; the fraction of time it is on (the "duty cycle", 0 to 1) sets the average power, and so the motor's speed.

from gpiozero import PWMOutputDevice
from time import sleep

motor = PWMOutputDevice(17)

for level in (0.4, 0.7, 1.0):
    motor.value = level
    sleep(0.5)

motor.off()

Line 1 — from gpiozero import PWMOutputDevice: imports the PWM-capable output object, which can hold any level between fully off and fully on.

Line 4 — motor = PWMOutputDevice(17): creates the PWM object on the same GPIO 17 driving the transistor.

Line 6 — for level in (0.4, 0.7, 1.0):: loops over three intensity levels — a gentle 40%, a firmer 70%, then full power.

Line 7 — motor.value = level: sets the duty cycle. At 0.4 the pin is on 40% of the time, so the motor gets roughly 40% of full power and buzzes more softly.

Line 8 — sleep(0.5): holds each level for half a second so you can feel the difference.

Line 10 — motor.off(): stops the motor cleanly at the end.

Note: a very small ERM motor may not turn at all below a certain level (say 0.3) because there is not enough power to overcome friction. Experiment to find the lowest level that reliably starts your salvaged motor, and start each pattern at or above it.

Reuse 3 — the Loudspeaker

The phone's tiny loudspeaker is genuinely reusable — but with an important catch you must know about the Pi 5. The Raspberry Pi 5 has no analog headphone jack. Earlier Pis had a 3.5 mm socket; the Pi 5 removed it. Audio now comes only over HDMI, USB, Bluetooth, or the I²S digital audio pins on the GPIO header. So you cannot simply solder a speaker to an audio jack — there is not one.

Careful — a common myth. Any tutorial that tells you to wire a speaker to the Pi 5's "audio jack" is out of date: the Pi 5 has none. Ignore that advice. We will use the modern, clean route instead: a small digital amplifier on the I²S pins.
In plain words: I²S (Inter-IC Sound) is a digital audio "language" spoken over a few GPIO pins. The Pi sends the sound as numbers; a small amplifier board turns those numbers into the analog waveform a speaker needs. A class-D amplifier does this very efficiently by rapidly switching, so it stays cool and sips power.

The recommended board is the MAX98357A — a tiny, cheap I²S class-D mono amplifier that connects directly to the GPIO header and drives a small 8 Ω speaker at up to about 1.8 W from 5 V. It needs only four signal connections plus power.

Enable I²S, then play a sound

The MAX98357A needs the Pi's I²S audio output switched on with a small device-tree overlay. On Raspberry Pi OS Bookworm, edit the firmware config and reboot:

sudo nano /boot/firmware/config.txt

sudo nano /boot/firmware/config.txt: opens the Pi's firmware configuration file in the nano text editor with administrator rights (sudo). On Bookworm this file lives at /boot/firmware/config.txt (older guides may say /boot/config.txt).

Find the line dtparam=audio=on and put a # in front of it to disable the old analog audio, then add the I²S overlay on a new line at the end of the file:

#dtparam=audio=on
dtoverlay=hifiberry-dac

Line 1 — #dtparam=audio=on: the leading # comments out (disables) the built-in audio so it does not clash with our I²S amplifier.

Line 2 — dtoverlay=hifiberry-dac: loads a generic I²S DAC overlay. The MAX98357A is electrically compatible with this widely-used overlay, so the Pi presents it as a normal sound card after a reboot. Save with Ctrl+O, exit with Ctrl+X, then run sudo reboot.

After rebooting, the amplifier appears as a normal audio device, so you can play sounds the ordinary way. Here is a tiny Python program that plays a WAV file by calling the system's aplay tool:

import subprocess

def play(path):
    subprocess.run(["aplay", path])

play("/usr/share/sounds/alsa/Front_Center.wav")

Line 1 — import subprocess: imports Python's subprocess module, which lets your program run other command-line programs and wait for them to finish.

Line 3 — def play(path):: wraps the playback in a function so you can reuse it for any file by name.

Line 4 — subprocess.run(["aplay", path]): runs the aplay command (the ALSA WAV player that ships with Raspberry Pi OS), passing the file path as its argument. Giving the command as a list of separate strings is the safe way to call external programs.

Line 6 — play(...): calls the function with a test sound that comes preinstalled, so you can confirm the amp works before supplying your own audio.

Note — be honest about the speaker. A salvaged phone speaker is a tiny driver: it is usually 8 Ω but rated only a fraction of a watt, with little bass and a modest maximum volume. The MAX98357A can output up to about 1.8 W, so do not drive it hard or you will hear distortion (and may damage the little driver). It is perfect for beeps, spoken alerts and gentle tunes — not for filling a room.
Tip: if you would rather use an analog amplifier such as a PAM8302, feed it from a cheap USB sound card (the Pi 5 has no analog line-out of its own). Analog amps are simple but can "pop" at start-up and pick up more noise; the I²S route above is cleaner for most projects.

Reuse 4 — the Whole Phone as a Wireless Camera

This is the big one — the reuse that makes the whole chapter worthwhile. You cannot harvest the phone's camera module, but you absolutely can use the phone's camera by leaving it exactly where it is, inside the working phone, and reading its video over Wi-Fi. This sidesteps every proprietary connector and missing-driver problem in one stroke.

The trick: install a free IP-webcam app on the phone (popular ones are IP Webcam, DroidCam, or Iriun). The app turns the phone into a little web server that streams its camera over your home network. Your Pi then reads that stream with OpenCV, the standard computer-vision library, in just a couple of lines.

Install OpenCV the Bookworm way

OpenCV is not preinstalled. Because Bookworm protects the system Python (the PEP 668 rule you met earlier), install it inside a virtual environment:

python3 -m venv ~/cvenv
source ~/cvenv/bin/activate
pip install opencv-python

Line 1 — python3 -m venv ~/cvenv: creates a private, self-contained Python environment in a folder called cvenv in your home folder, so anything you install stays separate from the system Python.

Line 2 — source ~/cvenv/bin/activate: switches your terminal into that environment; your prompt gains a (cvenv) prefix. Now pip installs inside the venv.

Line 3 — pip install opencv-python: downloads and installs OpenCV into the venv, with no PEP 668 error because you are no longer touching the system Python. (As a quicker alternative you can run sudo apt install python3-opencv system-wide.)

The frame-capture loop

Now the heart of computer vision: a frame-capture loop. Video is just a fast sequence of still images called frames. We open the stream once, then loop forever, reading one frame at a time and doing something with it (here, showing it in a window).

import cv2

cap = cv2.VideoCapture("http://PHONE-IP:8080/video")

while True:
    ret, frame = cap.read()
    if not ret:
        print("No frame - is the phone app running?")
        break
    cv2.imshow("Phone camera", frame)
    if cv2.waitKey(1) & 0xFF == ord("q"):
        break

cap.release()
cv2.destroyAllWindows()

Line 1 — import cv2: imports OpenCV. The module is named cv2 for historical reasons; it is the external library that does all the heavy video work for us.

Line 3 — cap = cv2.VideoCapture("http://PHONE-IP:8080/video"): opens the phone's video stream. Replace PHONE-IP with the address the app shows (for example 192.168.1.42); the IP Webcam app serves its MJPEG stream at the /video path on port 8080.

Line 5 — while True:: the frame-capture loop — it repeats forever until we deliberately break out of it.

Line 6 — ret, frame = cap.read(): grabs the next frame. read() hands back two things: ret, a True/False saying whether a frame arrived, and frame, the image itself (a grid of pixels).

Lines 7–9 — if not ret:: if no frame came through (the app stopped, the phone locked, or the network dropped), print a friendly message and leave the loop instead of crashing.

Line 10 — cv2.imshow("Phone camera", frame): shows the frame in a window titled "Phone camera". Each pass of the loop replaces it, so the stills blur into smooth video.

Line 11 — if cv2.waitKey(1) & 0xFF == ord("q"):: waits 1 millisecond for a key press; if you press q, it breaks the loop. This also gives the window time to redraw — without a waitKey the image never appears.

Lines 14–15 — cap.release() / cv2.destroyAllWindows(): tidy up after the loop: free the stream and close the window. Always release the capture when you are done.

Once you have frames flowing, you can do far more than display them. A small tweak saves a snapshot to disk instead of showing it:

import cv2

cap = cv2.VideoCapture("http://PHONE-IP:8080/video")
ret, frame = cap.read()
if ret:
    cv2.imwrite("snapshot.jpg", frame)
    print("Saved snapshot.jpg")
cap.release()

Line 4 — ret, frame = cap.read(): grabs a single frame (no loop this time).

Lines 5–7 — if ret: cv2.imwrite("snapshot.jpg", frame): if a frame arrived, cv2.imwrite writes it to disk as a JPEG image, and we print a confirmation. This is the basis of a time-lapse or a doorbell snapshot.

Line 8 — cap.release(): frees the stream straight away, since we only needed one frame.

Tip: these very same frame images can be fed to the AI vision and object-detection code from the earlier chapters — the phone simply becomes the eyes. Wherever those chapters used a Pi camera, you can hand them frames from this stream instead.
Careful — the real-world gotchas. Free app tiers often cap the resolution (commonly 640×480). Android may stop the camera when the screen locks or the app goes to the background, so keep the app in the foreground and the screen awake (and ideally the phone on charge). Usually only one program can read the stream at a time. And of course the phone and Pi must be on the same Wi-Fi network.

Other Useful Bits

Beyond the four headline reuses, a phone yields a few smaller treasures worth pocketing.

  • The microSD card — if the phone has a card slot, that card is a completely standard removable card. Pop it into the Pi's slot (after backing up anything you want to keep). It is the one piece of phone "storage" that genuinely transfers.
  • The buttons — the volume and power buttons are simple momentary switches. Desoldered (carefully), each can become a Button GPIO input with gpiozero's internal pull-up, exactly like the push button you wired earlier.
  • The USB-C port and cables — ready-made 5 V power leads and data cables. A phone charger cable is perfect for powering a project (just never open the charger brick — that is mains-side and dangerous, as the e-waste lesson warned).
  • The glass back and metal frame — surprisingly handsome raw material for a project enclosure. A salvaged frame makes a slim, sturdy case for a small Pi build.

Here is the button reuse in code — identical to wiring any tactile switch, because electrically that is all a phone button is:

from gpiozero import Button
from signal import pause

button = Button(27)

def pressed():
    print("Salvaged button pressed!")

button.when_pressed = pressed
pause()

Line 1 — from gpiozero import Button: imports the Button object, which automatically enables the Pi's internal pull-up resistor so you need no external parts.

Line 4 — button = Button(27): creates a button on GPIO 27 (physical pin 13). Wire one side of the salvaged switch to this pin and the other to GND.

Line 6 — def pressed():: defines a function to run each time the button is pressed.

Line 9 — button.when_pressed = pressed: tells gpiozero to call your pressed function whenever the switch closes. Note there are no parentheses — you hand over the function itself, not its result.

Line 10 — pause(): keeps the program alive and listening for presses.

What You Genuinely Can't Do — and Why

Let us close the loop on the myths, plainly, so you can confidently ignore the misleading tutorials.

You can't plug the phone camera into the Pi's camera port

The phone's camera connects over MIPI CSI-2, and the Pi's camera port also uses CSI-2 — so it looks like they should mate. They do not. CSI-2 is only the link standard (how the bits travel); it says nothing about the sensor's registers, its image-signal-processor (ISP) tuning, or its physical connector. Phone makers never publish the driver or tuning, and the board-to-board connectors are custom. With no driver and no matching plug, the camera is a dead end on a Pi.

You can't wire the AMOLED screen to the Pi's display port

Same story for the screen, which uses MIPI DSI. It needs precise power and backlight rails, a per-panel driver, and a tiny high-density connector that does not match the Pi's. Bringing one to life would need a rare, expensive matched driver board — firmly not a beginner project.

There is no "graphics card" to add to your Pi

Careful — a popular myth. You will see claims like "harvest the phone's GPU and add it to your Pi". This is simply impossible. A phone has no discrete or removable GPU. The graphics processor is a licensed design (an ARM Mali or Qualcomm Adreno core) fused permanently into the same piece of silicon as the CPU, inside the SoC. It cannot be unplugged, transplanted, or "added" to anything. There is literally nothing to remove.

And the soldered UFS storage, RAM and modem are proprietary BGA chips welded to the board — not reusable either. The one storage exception, remember, is a removable microSD card.

Safety & Disposal Checklist

Run through this list every single time you work with phone parts.

  1. Inspect the cell first. If the battery is swollen, hot, hissing, leaking or deformed, stop — do not use or charge it. Set it aside on a non-flammable surface for recycling.
  2. Never deform a cell. No puncturing, bending, crushing, drilling or shorting, ever. Keep the protection PCB attached.
  3. Charge attended. Salvaged cells charge only while you are present, on a ceramic tile or metal tin, never overnight, never on soft furnishings.
  4. Power off before wiring. Shut down and unplug the Pi before connecting the motor, amplifier or buttons; only re-power after a careful check.
  5. Mind static. Touch a metal surface to discharge yourself before handling bare boards and salvaged parts — static can quietly kill electronics. (This is "ESD", electrostatic discharge.)
  6. Never open mains chargers. Keep the USB-C cable, recycle the brick whole — its mains-side capacitors hold a dangerous charge.
  7. Recycle responsibly. Everything you cannot use — the board, screen, cameras, and especially any damaged battery — goes to a proper WEEE / battery recycling point, never the household bin.
Careful: a damaged lithium cell is the single biggest hazard in this whole chapter. If in any doubt about a battery's condition, do not use it. A purpose-built UPS module is always the safer choice, and there is no project worth a fire.

Make It Your Own / Where Next

You now have four genuine reuses and a clear-eyed view of the rest. Here are concrete ways to combine them into something bigger:

  • Give the robot eyes. Mount the phone on the Pi robot from the earlier chapter and feed its Wi-Fi stream into the navigation code — the phone becomes the robot's high-quality camera, no Pi camera needed.
  • A battery-backed headless server. Pair a (perfect-condition) cell or, better, a UPS HAT with a headless Pi running a small home server, so a brief power cut triggers a clean, safe shutdown instead of a corrupted card.
  • A doorbell camera. Combine the snapshot code with the salvaged button and a buzz from the vibration motor: press the button, the Pi grabs a frame from the phone-camera at the door and buzzes you a haptic alert.
  • A talking sensor node. Use the I²S speaker to have your Pi announce sensor readings aloud, and the vibration motor as a silent backup alert.

What You've Learned

This was an honest, safety-first salvage lesson. You now know:

  • the honest map of a phone — that the camera (proprietary MIPI CSI-2), AMOLED screen (MIPI DSI), the non-existent removable "GPU" (fused in the SoC), and the soldered SoC/RAM/UFS/modem are not practically reusable, and exactly why;
  • the genuine reuses — the microSD card, the Li-ion battery (via a protected TP4056 charger and a 5 V boost converter, with the honest Pi-5 peak-current caveat), the vibration motor (NPN transistor + base resistor + flyback diode), the loudspeaker (I²S MAX98357A, because the Pi 5 has no analog jack), the buttons, and the best of all, the whole phone as a Wi-Fi camera read with OpenCV;
  • the Python fundamentals — functions, loops, using external libraries (gpiozero and OpenCV), and a frame-capture loop;
  • the electronics fundamentals — lithium-ion safety & charging, the transistor motor driver with a flyback diode, a class-D I²S amplifier, and a boost converter;
  • and the rule that outranks them all: never deform a lithium-ion cell, charge attended, and choose a purpose-built UPS when in doubt.

That dead phone in the drawer was never a parts bin you could plug straight into a Pi — but with the right respect and the right helper boards, a few of its parts, and the whole device as a camera, can genuinely live again.
