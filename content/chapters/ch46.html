Lay a strip of black tape across a pale floor and watch a little robot hunt along it like a beetle following a scent trail. That is a line-following robot, and by the end of this chapter you will have built one from the same salvaged drive base you made in Lesson 44. You will learn to read cheap infra-red reflectance sensors with gpiozero's LineSensor, turn their readings into steering inside a control loop, and meet the single most important idea in all of robotics control: PID — starting with proportional steering and adding a damping term so your rover glides along the line instead of zig-zagging across it.

What You'll Build & Learn

A line follower is the perfect next project after the Junkbox AI Rover of Lesson 44, because almost all of the hardware is already on your chassis. You keep the two motors, the H-bridge driver and the separate motor battery exactly as they were. All you add is a small array of reflectance sensors pointing down at the floor, and a new, smarter steering loop. The result is a robot that needs no camera, no Wi-Fi and no marker — just a line on the ground — yet teaches a control idea used in everything from drones to thermostats to self-driving cars.

Python fundamentals (named)

  • Reading digital sensors with LineSensor — gpiozero's ready-made object for a single reflectance sensor. You will read its .value and .line_detected, and react to the line with the when_line event — the same event style you used for buttons.
  • The control loop — the while True: heartbeat from Lesson 44 that reads the sensors, decides, and steers, many times a second. Here it becomes the line-following brain.
  • PID control — the fundamental. We turn the sensor readings into a single error (how far off the line we are), multiply it by a gain to get a steering correction (the P, proportional term), then add a D (derivative) term that damps the wobble. This proportional-integral-derivative idea is the backbone of real-world control engineering.

Electronics fundamentals (named)

  • Infra-red reflectance sensing — the TCRT5000 module: an infra-red LED shining at the floor and a phototransistor reading how much bounces back. Black tape reflects little; pale floor reflects a lot.
  • Logic-safe powering — powering the sensor module from the Pi's 3.3 V pin so its digital output can never exceed 3.3 V and damage a GPIO input. We meet the on-board LM393 comparator and its trim-pot threshold.
  • Analogue vs digital, and why the Pi has no ADC — why the module's digital DO pin plugs straight into GPIO, but its analogue AO pin needs an extra chip the Pi simply does not have on board.
  • Reusing the H-bridge — the motors still run from their own battery through the driver from Lessons 42 and 44, with a common ground. A GPIO pin never drives a motor directly.
Note: You do not need a fancy kit. A pair of cheap TCRT5000 modules, or a ready-made two- or three-sensor line array, costs very little; the CamJam EduKit #3 robotics kit even ships one that gpiozero's LineSensor was written for. Where your parts differ, the code and the ideas still teach you everything. We always say "your robot" rather than assume a particular brand.

How a Reflectance Sensor Sees a Line

A line follower needs to answer one tiny question, over and over: am I looking at the line, or at the floor? The cheapest reliable way to ask it is a reflectance sensor.

In plain words: a reflectance sensor shines an invisible infra-red (IR) light downward and measures how much of it bounces back. A dark surface (black tape) absorbs most of the light, so little returns. A pale surface (white card, a light floor) reflects most of it back. By measuring the reflection, the sensor can tell dark from light without ever "seeing" colour the way your eye does.

The module almost everyone starts with — and the one gpiozero's LineSensor was designed around — is the TCRT5000. On a little breakout board it carries four parts working together:

  • An infra-red LED that shines IR light at the floor (you cannot see it, but a phone camera often can).
  • A phototransistor sitting right beside it that conducts more when more IR light lands on it.
  • An LM393 comparator — a chip that compares the phototransistor's reading against a threshold and snaps its output cleanly to HIGH or LOW.
  • A blue trim potentiometer (a tiny adjustable knob, the "trim-pot") that sets where that threshold sits. Turning it tunes how dark "dark" has to be.

The module has four pins, and getting them right is the whole game:

PinMeaningConnects to
VCCPower inPi 3.3 V (never 5 V — see the warning)
GND0 V referencePi GND (shared with everything)
DODigital out: HIGH or LOW, threshold set by the trim-potA Pi GPIO input — this is what LineSensor reads
AOAnalogue out: a smoothly varying voltageNothing, on a Pi — it would need an ADC chip

Digital DO versus analogue AO — and why the Pi has no ADC

The module gives you two different kinds of output, and understanding the difference explains a fundamental limit of the Raspberry Pi.

In plain words: an analogue signal can be any voltage in a range — like a dimmer dial that slides smoothly from 0 V to 3.3 V. A digital signal is only ever one of two values: LOW (near 0 V) or HIGH (near 3.3 V) — like a light switch that is only off or on. GPIO pins read digital signals.

The AO (analogue out) pin gives the raw reflection strength: a high voltage over pale floor, sliding down toward 0 V as the line passes underneath. That richness is useful — it tells you not just "dark or light" but how dark. The catch is that the Raspberry Pi 5, unlike an Arduino, has no analogue-to-digital converter (ADC) built in. Its GPIO pins can only tell HIGH from LOW; they cannot measure a voltage in between. To read AO you would need to add an external ADC chip such as the MCP3008, wired over the Pi's SPI bus.

The DO (digital out) pin avoids all that. The on-board LM393 comparator has already done the hard part: it compares the phototransistor's voltage against the threshold you dialled in with the trim-pot, and outputs a clean HIGH or LOW. That snaps straight onto a GPIO input with nothing in between — which is exactly what gpiozero's LineSensor expects. For a beginner line follower, use DO and forget AO exists.

Note: If you ever want the smoother control that AO gives — or you graduate to a Pololu QTR sensor array — you have two routes. The analogue QTR-A arrays need an MCP3008 ADC, exactly like AO above. The RC versions (QTR-RC) are clever: they are digital and need no ADC, but rely on precise microsecond timing instead. For this project we keep it simple with digital DO sensors.

Powering the Sensor Safely

This is the single most important wiring decision in the whole chapter, so we give it its own section.

Careful — the rules that protect your Pi:
  • Power off and unplug the Pi before wiring anything. Shut down from the desktop, wait for the lights to go out, then pull the lead. Re-power only after a full check.
  • Power the IR modules from 3.3 V, not 5 V. Connect each module's VCC to a Pi 3.3 V pin. The LM393's digital output swings up to whatever you feed VCC. At 3.3 V the DO line tops out at a GPIO-safe 3.3 V. Power the module at 5 V and DO can reach ~5 V — and feeding 5 V into a GPIO input will permanently destroy that pin. The Pi 5 has no protection fuse on its GPIO.
  • Keep the motors on their own supply. The two drive motors still run from their separate ~9 V battery through the H-bridge, exactly as in Lessons 42 and 44 — never from a Pi pin.
  • One common ground. Join the Pi GND, the sensors' GND, the H-bridge GND and the motor battery's minus terminal at a single 0 V rail. Without a shared ground, the Pi's "3.3 V HIGH" means nothing to the driver.

Why does powering at 3.3 V make DO safe? The LM393 comparator is an open output that gets "pulled up" toward the chip's supply voltage when it goes HIGH. Whatever you connect to VCC sets that ceiling. Feed it 3.3 V and HIGH is 3.3 V — precisely the logic level the Pi 5's GPIO inputs are built for. It is the easiest possible level-shift: pick the right supply and there is nothing else to do.

Tip: The Pi has two 3.3 V pins (physical pins 1 and 17) but several GND pins. With two or three sensors you will run short of 3.3 V holes — so feed 3.3 V to a breadboard power rail once, then take each sensor's VCC from that rail. Do the same with a GND rail.

Meet the LineSensor Object

gpiozero hides every detail of reading the DO pin behind one tidy object: LineSensor. You point it at a GPIO number and it does the rest. Before wiring the whole array, prove a single sensor works with this four-line program. Save it as one_sensor.py.

from gpiozero import LineSensor
from signal import pause

sensor = LineSensor(17)              # DO connected to GPIO 17 (physical pin 11)
sensor.when_line = lambda: print("On the line  (dark)")
sensor.when_no_line = lambda: print("Off the line (pale floor)")
pause()

Line 1 — from gpiozero import LineSensor: Pulls in gpiozero's ready-made object for a single-pin reflectance sensor. It is written for exactly the TCRT5000-style module you are using.

Line 2 — from signal import pause: Brings in pause(), which keeps an event-driven program alive and waiting. Without it the program would end instantly and you would never see a reading.

Line 4 — sensor = LineSensor(17): Creates the sensor object. The 17 is the GPIO (BCM) number — GPIO 17, which is physical pin 11 on the header — and it must be the pin wired to the module's DO output.

Line 5 — sensor.when_line = lambda: print(...): The when_line event runs the moment the sensor first sees the line (the dark surface). We hand it a tiny one-line function (a lambda) that prints a message. This is the same event style you used for the Button earlier in the course.

Line 6 — sensor.when_no_line = ...: The mirror event: it fires the moment the line disappears and the sensor sees pale floor again.

Line 7 — pause(): Sleeps forever so the two events can keep firing as you slide the sensor over the line by hand.

Run it, then slowly slide a strip of black tape under the sensor. You should see the two messages alternate. That confirms wiring, power and the trim-pot threshold are all correct.

Reading the value directly

Events are lovely for printing messages, but a control loop wants to ask the sensor what it sees, right now, on demand. LineSensor gives you two properties for that:

  • sensor.value — a number from 0 to 1. It is an average of the last few readings, so it is smooth. ~0 means the line (dark); ~1 means pale floor (light).
  • sensor.line_detected — a simple True/False: is the sensor over the line right now?
from gpiozero import LineSensor
from time import sleep

sensor = LineSensor(17)

while True:
    print(f"value = {sensor.value:.2f}   line_detected = {sensor.line_detected}")
    sleep(0.2)

Line 4 — sensor = LineSensor(17): Same sensor on GPIO 17.

Line 6 — while True:: A simple polling loop — the seed of the control loop we build later. It reads the sensor over and over.

Line 7 — sensor.value / sensor.line_detected: Prints both at once. The :.2f rounds the value to two decimals. Watch it fall toward 0.00 as the line slides under, and rise toward 1.00 over pale floor.

Line 8 — sleep(0.2): Waits a fifth of a second between prints so the output is readable. In the real control loop we will loop far faster than this.

Tip: One LineSensor object owns one pin. For a two- or three-sensor array you create one LineSensor per module, each on its own GPIO. We do exactly that next.

The Sensor Array Under the Chassis

A single sensor can follow a line, but only by weaving along one edge of it — it has to keep losing and re-finding the line to know which way to steer. Two or three sensors, mounted side by side under the front of the chassis, follow far more smoothly because at every instant they tell you which part of the line is underneath.

The logic is intuitive:

  • Only the centre sensor sees the line → you are centred → drive straight.
  • The left sensor sees the line → the line has drifted to your left → steer left to catch it.
  • The right sensor sees the line → steer right.
  • No sensor sees the line → you have lost it → stop, or spin gently to search.

Note — reusing the H-bridge: nothing about the motors changes from Lesson 44. The two motors still wire to the H-bridge outputs, the H-bridge logic inputs still take 3.3 V from GPIO 20/21 and 19/26, and the motors still draw from their own ~9 V battery at the driver's VM pin. A GPIO pin can never source the current a motor needs — that is the whole reason the H-bridge exists.

Mounting the array

  1. Mount the sensors low and forward. Fix the three modules under the front of the chassis, a few millimetres above the floor, facing straight down. Mounting them ahead of the wheels gives the rover a moment to react before the line drifts under the axle.
  2. Space them to straddle the line. Set the gap so that when the centre sensor sits on the line, the left and right sit just off it on pale floor. A spacing close to the line's own width works well.
  3. Keep stray light out. A small shroud (even black card) around the sensors stops room light and sunlight from confusing the phototransistor.
  4. Re-check the common ground. The sensors' GND, the Pi's GND, the H-bridge GND and the battery minus must all meet. This is the wire people forget.

A First Line Follower

Time to drive. We reuse the Robot class from Lesson 44 unchanged — save it as robot.py beside this program if it is not already there. Our new file creates three LineSensors and runs a control loop that turns their readings into steering. Save it as follow_simple.py, and prop the wheels off the ground for the first run.

# follow_simple.py  --  three-sensor "switch" line follower
from robot import Robot
from gpiozero import LineSensor
from time import sleep

robot = Robot(left_pins=(20, 21), right_pins=(19, 26))

left   = LineSensor(17)
centre = LineSensor(27)
right  = LineSensor(22)

CRUISE = 0.4        # forward speed when centred
TURN   = 0.3        # wheel speed while steering

print("Following. Ctrl+C to stop.")
try:
    while True:
        on_left   = left.line_detected
        on_centre = centre.line_detected
        on_right  = right.line_detected

        if on_centre and not on_left and not on_right:
            robot.forward(CRUISE)          # centred: go straight
        elif on_left:
            robot.turn_left(TURN)          # line drifted left
        elif on_right:
            robot.turn_right(TURN)         # line drifted right
        else:
            robot.stop()                   # lost the line: wait

        sleep(0.01)
except KeyboardInterrupt:
    robot.stop()
    print("\nStopped.")

Lines 2–4 — imports: the Robot drive base from our module, gpiozero's LineSensor, and sleep to pace the loop.

Line 6 — robot = Robot(...): Builds the drive base on the same pins as Lesson 44. Nothing about the motors has changed.

Lines 8–10 — three sensors: One LineSensor per module, each on its own GPIO — left on 17, centre on 27, right on 22, matching the wiring table. One object owns one pin.

Lines 12–13 — speeds: CRUISE is how fast we roll when centred; TURN is the wheel speed while steering. Start gentle; you can raise them later.

Line 18 — while True:: The control loop — read, decide, act, forever.

Lines 19–21 — read all three: line_detected gives a plain True/False for each sensor: is it over the dark line right now?

Lines 23–24 — go straight: If only the centre sensor is on the line, we are nicely centred, so drive forward.

Lines 25–28 — steer: If the left sensor catches the line, the line has slid to our left, so we turn left to chase it; the right sensor is the mirror case.

Lines 29–30 — lost it: If no sensor sees the line, we stop and wait rather than charge off blindly. (Later we make this a gentle search.)

Line 32 — sleep(0.01): A tiny pause so the loop runs about a hundred times a second — fast enough to react, without pointlessly hammering the CPU.

Lines 33–35 — clean stop: Ctrl+C always stops the motors, so the rover never keeps rolling after the program ends.

This works, and for a slow rover on a gently curving line it works well. But watch it on a tighter bend and you will see the problem: it lurches. It is either going dead straight or turning hard — there is no in between. The fix is the big idea of the chapter.

Introducing PID Control

The switch-style follower above has only three responses: straight, hard-left, hard-right. Real control engineering does something far smoother: it makes the correction proportional to how far off you are. A little off the line → a little steering. A lot off → a lot of steering. That is the P in PID, and it is the most important idea in this whole course's robotics strand.

In plain words: PID stands for Proportional–Integral–Derivative. It is a recipe used everywhere — cruise control, drones, 3D printers, thermostats — for steering a system back to where you want it. You measure an error (how far off you are), then compute a correction from three parts: P reacts to the error now, I reacts to error built up over time, and D reacts to how fast the error is changing. Add them up and that is your steering command.

Step 1: turn the sensors into one error number

To steer proportionally we first need a single number that says how far, and which way, we are off the line. We give each sensor a position weight and combine them:

  • Left sensor sits at position −1
  • Centre sensor at 0
  • Right sensor at +1

If only the centre sees the line, the error is 0 (perfectly centred). If the line has slid left, the left sensor reads dark, pulling the error negative. If it has slid right, the error goes positive. The further off, the bigger the number — exactly what proportional steering needs.

Step 2: proportional steering (P)

Once we have the error, the steering correction is just the error multiplied by a tuning number called the proportional gain, Kp:

correction = Kp * error

We then drive the two wheels at base_speed nudged in opposite directions by that correction: subtract it from one wheel and add it to the other, so the rover curves toward the line. A small Kp steers lazily; too large a Kp over-steers and the rover wobbles. P alone, well tuned, already follows a line beautifully — smoothly, not in lurches.

Step 3: damping the wobble (D)

The wobble that a strong P term causes comes from overshoot: the rover swings past the centre, then over-corrects back, again and again. The derivative term tames this. It looks at how fast the error is changing — the difference between this reading and the last — and pushes back against rapid change:

derivative  = error - last_error
correction  = Kp * error + Kd * derivative

When the rover is swinging back toward the line quickly, the error is shrinking fast, so the derivative is large and opposes the turn — easing off the steering early so the rover settles onto the line instead of sailing past it. That is "damping": the same effect as a door-closer that stops a door slamming.

Note — what about the I? The integral term adds up error over time to cancel a steady, persistent drift (say, one motor slightly weaker than the other). For a line follower it is rarely needed and is easy to mis-tune into a slow wobble of its own, so we leave Ki = 0 here and use just the PD pair. Naming all three keeps the fundamental honest: you are using PID, with one gain set to zero.

Now the full PD line follower. Save it as follow_pid.py.

# follow_pid.py  --  proportional-derivative line following
from robot import Robot
from gpiozero import LineSensor
from time import sleep

robot = Robot(left_pins=(20, 21), right_pins=(19, 26))

left   = LineSensor(17)
centre = LineSensor(27)
right  = LineSensor(22)

BASE = 0.45         # forward speed before steering
KP   = 0.6          # proportional gain  (start here, then tune)
KD   = 0.3          # derivative gain    (damps the wobble)

# sensor position weights: left pulls error negative, right positive
WEIGHTS = (-1.0, 0.0, 1.0)


def read_error(last_error):
    """Combine the three sensors into one signed line-position error."""
    darks = [1.0 - s.value for s in (left, centre, right)]   # 1 = on line
    total = sum(darks)
    if total == 0:
        return None                       # no sensor sees the line
    weighted = sum(w * d for w, d in zip(WEIGHTS, darks))
    return weighted / total


def clamp(x, low, high):
    return max(low, min(high, x))


print("PID following. Ctrl+C to stop.")
last_error = 0.0
try:
    while True:
        error = read_error(last_error)
        if error is None:
            robot.stop()                  # lost the line
            sleep(0.01)
            continue

        derivative = error - last_error
        correction = KP * error + KD * derivative
        last_error = error

        left_speed  = clamp(BASE + correction, 0, 1)
        right_speed = clamp(BASE - correction, 0, 1)
        robot.left.forward(left_speed)
        robot.right.forward(right_speed)

        sleep(0.01)
except KeyboardInterrupt:
    robot.stop()
    print("\nStopped.")

Lines 8–10 — three sensors: the same array as before, one LineSensor per GPIO.

Lines 12–14 — the gains: BASE is the cruising speed; KP and KD are the proportional and derivative gains you will tune. Tuning these two numbers is the heart of making a line follower good.

Line 17 — WEIGHTS: the position of each sensor: left = −1, centre = 0, right = +1. These turn "which sensor is dark" into "which way, and how far".

Line 20 — def read_error(...): the function that produces the single error number. Keeping it separate makes the loop read cleanly.

Line 22 — darks = [1.0 - s.value for s in ...]: remember value is ~0 on the dark line and ~1 on pale floor, so 1.0 - value flips it to "darkness": ~1 means on the line. Using value rather than line_detected gives a smooth number, not just yes/no.

Lines 23–25 — nothing seen: if every sensor is on pale floor the total darkness is 0, so we return None to mean "line lost".

Lines 26–27 — the weighted average: multiply each sensor's darkness by its position weight, sum, and divide by the total darkness. The result is a centre-of-darkness: 0 when centred, negative when the line is left, positive when right.

Lines 30–31 — clamp: a small helper to keep a wheel speed inside the valid 0–1 range, so a big correction cannot ask for an impossible speed.

Line 38 — error = read_error(last_error): read the current line-position error at the top of every loop.

Lines 39–42 — line lost: if there is no line, stop and skip the rest of this pass.

Line 44 — derivative = error - last_error: the D term's raw material — how much the error changed since last loop. A fast swing back gives a large derivative.

Line 45 — correction = KP * error + KD * derivative: the PID formula itself (with Ki = 0). P reacts to where we are; D reacts to how fast we are getting there.

Line 46 — last_error = error: remember this error for the next loop's derivative. Forgetting this line is the classic PID bug.

Lines 48–49 — mix into wheel speeds: add the correction to one wheel and subtract it from the other so the rover curves. A positive error (line on the right) speeds the left wheel and slows the right, turning the rover right — back toward the line.

Lines 50–51 — drive directly: we reach into the Robot's two Motor objects to set each wheel's speed independently — finer control than the named forward/turn helpers give.

Line 53 — sleep(0.01): a steady loop interval. Keeping the interval constant matters for the derivative, which assumes each loop is roughly the same length.

Tip — tune in order: set KD = 0 first and raise KP until the rover follows but wobbles a little. Then raise KD just enough to calm the wobble. If it still oscillates, lower KP a touch. Change one gain at a time and only by small steps.

The Camera Alternative

If you already fitted the camera from Lesson 44, you can follow a line with no reflectance sensors at all — the same OpenCV skills, pointed at the floor. The idea mirrors the colour-tracking pipeline you built before, but simpler: we only need to find where the dark line sits, left to right, in a strip near the bottom of the image.

The pipeline is: grab a frame → crop a thin region of interest (ROI) near the bottom (the floor just ahead of the rover) → convert to grey → threshold so the dark line becomes white and everything else black → find the white blob's centroid with cv2.moments → turn its x-position into the same error number, and feed it to the very same PID. Save this as follow_camera.py.

# follow_camera.py  --  follow a line using the camera (reuses Lesson 44 OpenCV)
import cv2
from robot import Robot
from time import sleep

robot = Robot(left_pins=(20, 21), right_pins=(19, 26))
cap = cv2.VideoCapture("http://PHONE-IP:8080/video")   # or a Pi Camera index

BASE, KP, KD = 0.45, 0.004, 0.002      # note: error is in pixels, so small gains


def line_error(frame):
    """Return the line's horizontal offset from centre, in pixels, or None."""
    h, w = frame.shape[:2]
    roi = frame[int(h * 0.8):h, 0:w]            # bottom 20% strip of floor
    grey = cv2.cvtColor(roi, cv2.COLOR_BGR2GRAY)
    # dark line -> white blob; THRESH_BINARY_INV flips dark to bright
    _, mask = cv2.threshold(grey, 60, 255, cv2.THRESH_BINARY_INV)
    M = cv2.moments(mask)
    if M["m00"] == 0:
        return None                             # no line in the strip
    cx = int(M["m10"] / M["m00"])
    return cx - (w // 2)                         # +ve = line is right of centre


print("Camera following. q in window or Ctrl+C to stop.")
last_error = 0.0
try:
    while True:
        ret, frame = cap.read()
        if not ret:
            continue
        err = line_error(frame)
        if err is None:
            robot.stop()
            continue
        derivative = err - last_error
        correction = KP * err + KD * derivative
        last_error = err
        robot.left.forward(max(0, min(1, BASE + correction)))
        robot.right.forward(max(0, min(1, BASE - correction)))
        sleep(0.01)
except KeyboardInterrupt:
    robot.stop()
    print("\nStopped.")

Line 7 — cap = cv2.VideoCapture(...): open the camera exactly as in Lesson 44 — the phone's Wi-Fi stream, or a Pi Camera index such as 0.

Line 9 — gains: here the error is measured in pixels (it can be tens or hundreds), so the gains are far smaller than the sensor version. The PID maths is identical; only the scale of the numbers differs.

Line 14 — h, w = frame.shape[:2]: the frame's height and width in pixels, so the crop and centre work for any resolution.

Line 15 — roi = frame[int(h*0.8):h, 0:w]: crop the bottom 20% — the patch of floor just in front of the rover. Looking only there ignores clutter higher in the frame and is faster.

Line 16 — cv2.cvtColor(... COLOR_BGR2GRAY): convert to greyscale; for a black line on a pale floor, brightness alone is enough — we do not need colour.

Line 18 — cv2.threshold(grey, 60, 255, THRESH_BINARY_INV): any pixel darker than 60 becomes white (255), everything else black — so the dark line turns into a bright blob. _INV is the "invert" that makes dark bright.

Lines 19–22 — centroid: cv2.moments of the mask gives m00 (the blob's area) and m10 (sum of x-positions). If m00 is 0 there is no line; otherwise m10/m00 is the line's centre x — the same centroid trick as Lesson 44's colour tracker.

Line 23 — return cx - (w // 2): subtract the frame's middle so we get a signed error: negative if the line is left of centre, positive if right — exactly the error our PID expects.

Lines 37–41 — the same PID: identical to the sensor version — derivative, correction, then mix into the two wheels. One line follower, two ways to measure the error.

Note: reading and decoding a Wi-Fi video stream costs time — expect roughly 10–20 frames a second on a Pi 5, as in Lesson 44. That is fine for a gentle follower, but it is why we keep BASE low: the rover should not move far between frames.

Calibration and Troubleshooting

Most line-follower trouble is not code — it is the sensor threshold. Calibrate first, then debug.

Calibrating with the trim-pot

  1. Run the value reader. Use the while True printing program from earlier, watching one sensor at a time.
  2. Hold the sensor over pale floor. The value should read close to 1.0 and the module's signal LED (most boards have one) should be off.
  3. Slide it over the black line. The value should drop close to 0.0 and the LED should light. If it does not switch, turn the blue trim-pot a little.
  4. Find the middle. Adjust the pot until there is a clear, reliable switch between line and floor with a comfortable margin either side. Tighten nothing — just leave it where it switches cleanly.
  5. Repeat for every sensor. Each module has its own pot and may need a slightly different setting, especially under uneven light.
SymptomLikely causeFix

Make It Your Own

You now have a line follower built on the fundamental of PID control. Here are four ways to push it further:

  • Tune for speed. Raise BASE gradually. A faster rover overshoots more, so you will need to re-tune: usually a touch less KP and a touch more KD. See how fast you can go before it leaves the line on the tightest bend.
  • Add the integral term. If your rover consistently rides slightly to one side, introduce a small KI: keep a running error_sum += error and add KI * error_sum to the correction. Start tiny — the I term is the easiest to over-do — and "clamp" the sum so it cannot grow without bound.
  • Search instead of stopping. When the line is lost, instead of stopping, spin slowly in the direction of the last non-zero error to hunt for it again — a robot that recovers from losing the line feels far more alive.
  • Go to five sensors. More sensors give a finer error (weights like −2, −1, 0, +1, +2), so steering is smoother still — or graduate to a Pololu QTR array (with an MCP3008 ADC for the analogue version) for a near-continuous read of the line's position.
From the e-waste pile: the TCRT5000 (and its cousins the CNY70 and many printer "paper-present" sensors) turns up inside dead inkjet printers, old computer mice with mechanical wheels, and broken toys. Salvaged reflectance sensors rarely come on a tidy breakout board, so you would add your own LM393 comparator — but the IR LED-and-phototransistor pair at the heart of them is exactly what you have been using here.

From three cheap sensors and one tuning loop you have built a robot that follows a path on its own — and met PID, the control idea that quietly steers a huge slice of the modern world.
