Welcome to your Raspberry Pi 5 — a complete, fully functional computer that fits in the palm of your hand. In this opening chapter you will find out what this little board actually is, why millions of people around the world use one, and which accessories you need to get started — whether you bought a starter kit or are gathering the pieces yourself. By the end, you will understand what you have in your hands and feel genuinely ready to start exploring.

What you need to get started

Before anything else, let's make sure you know what each item is and what it does. Many people start with a Raspberry Pi 5 "starter kit" that bundles most of these together; others buy the board and add accessories one by one. Either way works. Gather (or check off) each of the following:

Item What it is Why it matters
Raspberry Pi 5 board The main computer — a green or red circuit board roughly the size of a bank card. It comes in 2 GB, 4 GB, 8 GB, and 16 GB RAM versions. This is the heart of everything. All other items plug into or around it. Any RAM version works for everything in this book.
Active Cooler A small aluminium heatsink with a tiny fan that clips on top of the main chip. Keeps the processor cool during demanding tasks so it never has to slow itself down. Many starter kits include one already fitted; if yours didn't, the official Active Cooler is an inexpensive add-on that clips straight on.
Case A protective enclosure — often a smart aluminium box or a plastic shell. Protects the board from bumps and dust. Most kits include one (sometimes with the Pi already installed inside). A case is optional but recommended.
Power supply A mains plug with a USB-C cable. The official 27 W supply is rated 5.1 V / 5 A. Powers the Pi. The Pi 5 needs more power than older models, so use the official 27 W USB-C power supply (or any good 5 V / 5 A USB-C PD supply). Do not substitute a typical phone charger.
microSD card (and a card reader) A tiny memory card (commonly 32 GB or larger), plus a way to read it on your main computer — many cards come with a small USB adapter, or your computer may have a built-in SD slot. The microSD card is the Pi's main storage — it holds the operating system and all your files. Many kits include one, sometimes with Raspberry Pi OS already installed.
Micro-HDMI to HDMI cable A video cable with a small connector at one end (for the Pi) and a full-size connector at the other (for your TV or monitor). Many kits include one; otherwise it is a cheap, widely available cable. Sends the picture and sound from the Pi to your screen. Note the Pi end is micro-HDMI — this connector is smaller than the standard HDMI you find on TVs. You cannot use a standard HDMI cable directly; you need a micro-HDMI-to-HDMI cable or adapter.
Note: You will need to supply your own keyboard, mouse, and monitor (or TV with an HDMI input). Chapter 2 covers exactly which types work and how to connect everything up.
Tip: Check whether your microSD card already has Raspberry Pi OS installed. Look at the card label, or look for a sticker on the packaging saying "pre-loaded" or "pre-flashed". If it is pre-loaded, Chapter 2 will walk you straight to first boot. If it is blank — or you don't have a card yet — Chapter 3 covers installing the operating system yourself — it takes about ten minutes.

What exactly is a Raspberry Pi?

A Raspberry Pi is a single-board computer — every component that makes up a full computer (the processor, memory, graphics, USB ports, Wi-Fi, and more) is soldered directly onto one small circuit board roughly the size of a bank card. There is no separate tower, no desktop box, no jumble of internal cables. It is just one neat, tidy board.

In plain words: A "single-board computer" (often shortened to SBC) simply means that everything a computer needs — its brain, memory, and connections to the outside world — all live on a single flat piece of circuit board, rather than being spread across separate components inside a big box.

When you power it on, it boots an operating system, shows a desktop on your screen, connects to the internet, plays videos, browses the web, runs programs, and does essentially everything a normal laptop or desktop computer can do — just in a much smaller, more energy-efficient, and far cheaper package.

Note: The Pi 5 has no 3.5 mm headphone socket. If you want to hear audio, connect your screen or TV via the micro-HDMI cable (most modern monitors and all TVs carry sound over HDMI) or plug in USB speakers or a USB audio adapter. Bluetooth headphones and speakers also work perfectly.

A brief, friendly history

The Raspberry Pi was created by the Raspberry Pi Foundation, a British educational charity. Back in 2011, a group of academics and engineers in Cambridge, England, noticed a worrying trend: fewer and fewer school-leavers were arriving at university with any real experience of programming or electronics. Kids in the 1980s had grown up with BBC Micros and Commodore 64s — machines you had to program, because there was little else to do with them. By 2010, most children only knew how to use apps on someone else's device.

Their solution was radical in its simplicity: build an affordable, programmable computer — one cheap enough that a school or a child could actually buy one, tough enough to survive a backpack, and open enough that curious people could take it apart and put it back together in software without worrying about breaking anything expensive.

The first Raspberry Pi (the Model B) launched in February 2012 and sold 100,000 units in the first day. It cost $35. People queued online for hours to order one. The Foundation expected to sell a few thousand to enthusiastic teachers; they ended up starting a global movement.

Since then, the Foundation has sold well over 60 million boards across six main generations. Every penny of profit goes back into the charity's educational mission: funding projects, writing free learning materials, and training teachers worldwide. When you buy a Raspberry Pi, you are supporting that mission.

The Raspberry Pi 5, released in October 2023, is the most powerful yet. It is two to three times faster than the Pi 4 thanks to a brand-new processor, adds a proper PCIe connector for fast storage, and finally has a real power button. It comes in 2 GB, 4 GB, 8 GB, and 16 GB versions; even the smaller ones are far more capable than many budget laptops, and the 16 GB model is the top-of-the-range configuration.

What can you actually do with one?

This is where things get exciting. Because the Pi runs a full Linux operating system and has physical pins you can connect to real-world electronics, the range of things people make with it is genuinely astonishing. Here is just a small taste:

  • Media centre: Connect it to your TV and run Kodi or Jellyfin to stream your film and music collection — or watch YouTube and browse catch-up TV. It replaces a £100+ streaming box.
  • Retro gaming console: Install RetroPie or Batocera, plug in a USB gamepad, and play thousands of classic arcade, NES, SNES, Mega Drive, and PlayStation 1 games using your own ROM files.
  • Ad-blocker for your whole house: Run Pi-hole, a piece of software that intercepts advertising requests across your entire home network — every device benefits, including phones and smart TVs.
  • Home server / NAS: Attach a USB hard drive and run Nextcloud to create your own private "cloud" — photos, documents, and videos accessible from anywhere, with no monthly subscription.
  • Weather station: Connect temperature, humidity, pressure, and air-quality sensors to the GPIO pins and log real readings from your garden, displayed on a web page you build yourself.
  • Robot: Wire up motors, sensors, and a camera to build a moving robot you control from your phone or program to navigate on its own.
  • Smart-home hub: Run Home Assistant to control smart plugs, lights, heating, and security cameras — without sending any data to a third-party cloud.
  • AI assistant: Run a local large-language model (a "chatbot" that runs entirely on your own hardware, offline, with no API costs) using software such as Ollama. Smaller models run on a 4 GB or 8 GB Pi, while an 8 GB or 16 GB Pi 5 can run surprisingly capable AI models.
  • Web server: Host a real website — a personal blog, a family photo gallery, or a small business site — directly from your Pi, visible on the public internet.
  • Learn-to-code machine: The Pi ships with Python, Scratch, and dozens of other tools pre-installed. It is arguably the best beginner coding platform ever made, because you can connect your code to real physical things.
  • Security camera system: Mount a Pi Camera, run MotionEyeOS or Frigate, and record motion-triggered footage to a drive — a complete home security setup for the cost of the hardware.
  • Print server: Plug in an old USB printer that no longer has up-to-date drivers for modern computers, and the Pi will make it work wirelessly for every device in your house using CUPS.
  • VPN server: Run WireGuard so that your phone and laptop connect securely to your home network when you are abroad, as if you were sitting at home.
Tip: You do not need to know how to do any of these things yet. This book will walk you through many of them step by step. The point right now is simply to appreciate that the small board in front of you is capable of an extraordinary range of tasks.

How is it different from your everyday computer?

You probably use a laptop or desktop every day — most likely running Windows, macOS, or Linux — so it is worth understanding clearly how the Raspberry Pi is different, and why those differences actually matter.

The operating system: Linux, not Windows

The biggest practical difference for you as a newcomer is the operating system. Most everyday computers run Windows (made by Microsoft) or macOS (made by Apple). The Raspberry Pi runs Linux — a separate family of operating systems that is free, open-source, and used everywhere from web servers to Android phones to supercomputers.

In plain words: "Open-source" means the underlying code that makes the operating system work is publicly available for anyone to read, modify, and share. Nobody owns Linux in the way Microsoft owns Windows. It is maintained by a worldwide community of volunteers and companies working together.

The specific flavour of Linux on your Pi is called Raspberry Pi OS, currently nicknamed "Bookworm". Linux versions are traditionally given friendly codenames, and Bookworm is based on a widely-used Linux distribution called Debian 12. It looks and feels like a proper desktop: there is a taskbar, a desktop, a file manager, a web browser, and menus. If you have ever used a computer, you will recognise most of it within a few minutes.

In plain words: "Bookworm" is just a nickname — it doesn't mean anything technical. Linux and Debian releases are traditionally named after characters from Toy Story. "Bookworm" is Debian version 12, released in 2023. The version after it (Debian 13, called "Trixie") is being developed, but Bookworm is what ships with the Pi 5 and what all instructions in this book are written for.

The main difference you will notice is that many tasks that required clicking through installer windows on Windows or macOS are done with a single short command typed into the terminal — a text window where you give the computer instructions in plain text. This sounds intimidating, but it is actually faster and more powerful once you get used to it. Every terminal command in this book is explained line by line, so you will always know exactly what you are typing before you press Enter.

The processor: ARM, not x86

Most Windows PCs use an Intel or AMD processor that belongs to a family called x86-64. The Raspberry Pi 5 uses a completely different family called ARM (specifically, four Arm Cortex-A76 cores running at 2.4 GHz). ARM chips are designed to be very efficient — they do a lot of computing work per watt of electricity — which is why they are in every smartphone in the world, and now in Apple's Mac computers too.

For you as a user, this mostly does not matter. Software for Raspberry Pi OS is already compiled (prepared) for ARM, so you just install it normally. The practical upside is that your Pi sips electricity: running 24 hours a day, 365 days a year, it costs roughly £5–£10 in electricity — whereas a typical desktop PC left running constantly would cost £100 or more.

GPIO pins: connecting to the real world

Along the top edge of your Pi 5 is a row of 40 small metal pins — the GPIO header. GPIO stands for General-Purpose Input/Output. These pins let you connect your Pi directly to the physical world: sensors that detect temperature or light, motors that spin wheels, LEDs that flash, buttons you press, and much more.

In plain words: GPIO (General-Purpose Input/Output) pins are tiny metal contacts on the Pi that you can connect wires to. You can tell each pin to either send an electrical signal (to switch something on) or read an incoming signal (to detect whether something is happening). They operate at 3.3 volts — gentle enough to be safe for the electronics, but powerful enough to control a huge range of devices.
Careful: The GPIO pins on the Pi 5 operate at 3.3 volts. Connecting a signal that is higher than 3.3 V (for example, a 5 V signal from some Arduino components) can permanently damage the board. Always check the voltage of any component before wiring it to the GPIO header. The chapters on GPIO go slowly and carefully through this.

A normal laptop or desktop has no equivalent of this. It is one of the features that makes the Raspberry Pi special: your programs can reach out and touch the physical world, not just live inside a screen.

Why the Pi 5 is a big leap — and what more RAM unlocks

Every new generation of Raspberry Pi has brought meaningful improvements, but the jump from Pi 4 to Pi 5 is particularly significant.

What is new in the Pi 5

  • New processor: The BCM2712 chip uses four Arm Cortex-A76 cores running at 2.4 GHz. This is approximately 2–3× faster than the Pi 4's cores in real-world tasks.
  • VideoCore VII GPU: The graphics processor supports hardware video decoding up to 4K at 60 frames per second, making it a genuinely capable media machine.
  • Dual 4K displays: Two micro-HDMI ports mean you can plug in two monitors and run a proper multi-screen workspace. Note that both ports use the smaller micro-HDMI connector, so you need a micro-HDMI-to-HDMI cable or adapter (often included in starter kits).
  • PCIe 2.0 connector: A dedicated expansion slot lets you connect a fast NVMe SSD (solid-state storage drive) using an optional add-on board, making the Pi 5 dramatically faster than relying on a microSD card alone.
  • Real-time clock: A battery connector on the board lets it keep the correct time even when switched off — the Pi 4 forgot the time every time it lost power.
  • Physical power button: A dedicated button on the board lets you safely turn the Pi on and off. Holding it for two seconds triggers a clean shutdown; a brief press wakes it from sleep. This replaces the old approach of just pulling the power cable — which could corrupt your microSD card.
  • Improved USB and networking: USB 3.0 ports are now on a dedicated controller, meaning plugging in a USB hard drive no longer slows down the network — a known limitation of the Pi 4.
  • Active Cooler support: The Pi 5 has dedicated mounting points and a connector for the official Active Cooler (included with many starter kits, and an inexpensive add-on otherwise), which keeps performance high during intensive tasks without slowing down the chip.

Why the amount of RAM matters

In plain words: RAM (Random-Access Memory) is your computer's short-term working space. Think of it as a desk: the bigger the desk, the more papers (tasks and data) you can have open at once without having to put things away and fetch them back. More RAM means more things running simultaneously without the system slowing down.

The Raspberry Pi 5 is available with 2 GB, 4 GB, 8 GB, or 16 GB of RAM. Everything in this book works on any of them; the more RAM your board has, the more ambitious you can be. Here is what extra RAM unlocks:

  • Heavy multitasking: With 8 GB or more you can have a web browser with many tabs open, a document editor, a music player, and a running server — all at the same time — without the system slowing to a crawl.
  • Desktop use as a daily driver: With 8 GB or 16 GB, the Pi 5 is comfortable running the same kinds of tasks you do on a budget laptop. Many people use it as their main computer.
  • Running local AI models: Software like Ollama can load AI language models entirely into RAM and run them offline. With 2 GB or 4 GB you are limited to very small models; with 8 GB or 16 GB you can run genuinely useful models that can answer questions, summarise text, write code, and more — all on your own hardware, privately.
  • Running several services simultaneously: If you want your Pi to be a media server and an ad-blocker and a home automation hub all at once, more RAM gives each service plenty of breathing room.
  • Compiling and building software: Tasks like building programs from source code require holding a lot of data in RAM at once. More RAM makes this comfortable rather than agonisingly slow.
Note: In everyday use — browsing the web, writing documents, watching videos — you will not see a dramatic difference between, say, 4 GB and 8 GB. The larger models shine when you start stacking up ambitious projects, running multiple services, or experimenting with AI. If you are choosing a board, more RAM is good future-proofing — but whatever version you have, the projects in this book are written to work on it.

You almost certainly cannot break it

If you are nervous about experimenting, here is the single most reassuring thing we can tell you:

In software, almost everything is reversible. If you install something and it goes wrong, you can uninstall it. If you accidentally change a setting that breaks something, you can change it back — or look up how to fix it, and this book will help. If things get truly confused and you cannot work out what happened, you can wipe the microSD card and re-install the operating system in about ten minutes, starting with a completely fresh system as if the Pi just came out of the box. You cannot physically damage the processor or memory by running the wrong command.

The only hardware risks to be aware of are:

  • Connecting the wrong voltage to GPIO pins could damage the board — but we will always tell you clearly what voltages to use, and the chapters on GPIO go slowly and carefully.
  • Pulling the power cable out while the Pi is writing to the microSD card can sometimes corrupt the card — but this is rare, and again, a re-flash fixes it. Always use the power button or the proper shut-down option from the menu to switch off safely.
Tip: Experimenting is not just allowed — it is the whole point. The Raspberry Pi was created precisely so that people would poke around, try things, break things, and learn from fixing them. Every professional developer and engineer in the world learned by making mistakes. Make them freely.

How to use this book

This book is designed to be read chapter by chapter, in order, at least the first time through. Each chapter builds gently on what came before. Later, once you are comfortable, you can jump directly to whichever topic interests you — each chapter is also written to stand on its own as a reference.

Here are a few suggestions for getting the most from it:

  • Have your Pi in front of you. The best learning happens when you follow each step in real time on your own machine. Do not just read — type the commands, press the buttons, and see what happens.
  • Read the "explain" boxes under every code block. Immediately after every block of terminal commands or program code, there is a plain-English explanation of what each line does. Never type a command you do not understand — read the explanation first.
  • Do not skip the callout boxes. The coloured boxes marked Note, Tip, Careful, and "In plain words" contain important safety information, helpful shortcuts, and definitions of technical terms. They are there for a reason.
  • It is fine to go slowly. There is no test, no deadline, and no instructor watching the clock. If something is not clear, re-read it. If it still is not clear, try it on the Pi — practical experience often explains things better than words can.
  • Use the Key terms section at the end of each chapter. If you forget what a word means, it is defined there. There is also a glossary at the back of the book covering the most common terms across all chapters.
Note: Every command in this book has been written and tested for Raspberry Pi OS Bookworm (64-bit), which is the current version for the Pi 5 and the one most kits and downloads provide. If you are using a different version, some details may differ slightly.

Key terms

  • Single-board computer (SBC) — A complete computer built onto a single flat circuit board, with no separate components.
  • Raspberry Pi Foundation — The British educational charity that designs and sells Raspberry Pi computers, using profits to fund computing education worldwide.
  • ARM — A family of processor designs used in smartphones, tablets, and the Raspberry Pi; known for being very power-efficient.
  • x86-64 — The processor family used in most Windows PCs and laptops, made by Intel or AMD.
  • Linux — A free, open-source operating system used on the Raspberry Pi, web servers, Android phones, and supercomputers.
  • Raspberry Pi OS — The official Linux-based operating system for Raspberry Pi, currently codenamed "Bookworm" (based on Debian 12).
  • Bookworm — The codename for Debian 12, the Linux version that Raspberry Pi OS is currently based on. All instructions in this book apply to Bookworm.
  • GPIO (General-Purpose Input/Output) — The 40-pin connector on the Pi that lets you connect it to sensors, LEDs, motors, and other electronics. Operates at 3.3 V.
  • RAM (Random-Access Memory) — The short-term working memory of a computer; more RAM means more tasks can run simultaneously without slowdown.
  • PCIe — A high-speed expansion connector on the Pi 5 that allows you to attach a fast NVMe SSD storage drive via an add-on board.
  • microSD card — A tiny removable memory card, about the size of a fingernail, used as the main storage for the Raspberry Pi's operating system and files.
  • micro-HDMI — A smaller version of the HDMI video connector, used on the Raspberry Pi 5. You need a micro-HDMI to HDMI cable or adapter to connect to a standard monitor or TV.
  • Terminal — A text-based window where you type commands directly to the operating system; faster and more powerful than menus for many tasks.
  • Open-source — Software whose underlying code is publicly available for anyone to read, modify, and redistribute freely.
  • NVMe SSD — A type of very fast solid-state storage drive that can be connected to the Pi 5 via its PCIe slot using a suitable add-on board.
  • Active Cooler — The official Raspberry Pi heatsink-and-fan combination that clips onto the Pi 5 chip to keep it cool under load. Included with many starter kits, and an inexpensive add-on otherwise.
