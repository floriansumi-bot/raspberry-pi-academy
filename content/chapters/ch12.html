The Raspberry Pi has a secret superpower hidden along its top edge: a row of 40 metal pins that can talk directly to the real world — switching lights on and off, reading button presses, measuring temperature, detecting motion, and far more. In this chapter you will learn what those pins do, understand just enough electricity to use them safely, and build your first two real electronic projects from scratch.

What Is the GPIO Header?

Along the top edge of your Pi 5 board you will see two parallel rows of tiny metal pins — 20 in each row, 40 in total. This connector is called the GPIO header.

In plain words: GPIO stands for General-Purpose Input/Output. "General-purpose" means each pin can be used for many different jobs. "Input" means the Pi can sense something connected to it (a button, a sensor). "Output" means the Pi can control something (an LED, a buzzer, a motor driver).

Not every pin does the same thing. The 40 pins are divided into three groups:

  • Power pins — supply electricity to your components. There are two 5 V pins and two 3.3 V pins.
  • Ground (GND) pins — the return path for electricity. Think of these as the negative terminal of a battery. There are eight GND pins spread around the header for convenience.
  • GPIO pins — the programmable ones. Your Python code can set each of these HIGH (3.3 V) or LOW (0 V), or read whether something connected to them is HIGH or LOW.
Careful: The GPIO pins on the Raspberry Pi 5 operate at 3.3 V logic. If you connect anything that pushes 5 V into a GPIO input pin, you will permanently damage the Pi 5 — there is no fuse to protect it. Always use 3.3 V-compatible components on GPIO pins, or use a level-shifter circuit when you must interface with 5 V devices. Also, never let two pins touch each other (a "short circuit") — this can instantly destroy the board. Handle the header gently and double-check your wiring before powering up.

The 40-Pin Pinout

The diagram below shows every pin. The two columns of numbers are the physical pin numbers (the position on the header, 1–40). The labels beside them show what each pin does. Pin 1 is nearest the corner of the board marked with a tiny triangle on most Pi boards.

Pin 1 also usually has a slightly squared-off pad on the underside of the board.

Electricity for Absolute Beginners

You do not need an engineering degree to use GPIO safely, but understanding three basic ideas — voltage, current, and resistance — will stop you damaging things.

The Water Analogy

Imagine electricity flowing through a wire just like water flowing through a pipe.

  • Voltage (measured in Volts, V) is the pressure pushing the water through the pipe. A higher voltage pushes harder. Your Pi's GPIO pins provide 3.3 V "pressure".
  • Current (measured in Amperes, A, or milliamps, mA) is how much water is actually flowing per second. Too much current through a small wire (or a GPIO pin) will overheat it.
  • Resistance (measured in Ohms, Ω) is how narrow the pipe is — it slows the flow down. Adding resistance reduces the current.

These three are tied together by Ohm's Law: Current = Voltage ÷ Resistance. If you know two of the values, you can always work out the third. You will use this to calculate safe resistor values.

In plain words: An LED (Light-Emitting Diode) is a tiny lamp that only lets electricity flow in one direction. It lights up when current flows through it from its longer leg (the positive side, called the anode) to its shorter leg (the negative side, called the cathode). Fitting one in backwards means no light — but also no damage.

Why Every LED Needs a Resistor

An LED has almost no resistance of its own. If you connect it directly to 3.3 V and GND without anything to slow the current down, too much electricity flows through it, and it burns out immediately — sometimes taking the GPIO pin with it.

A series resistor is placed in the same wire as the LED to limit the current to a safe level. "Series" just means in a chain, one after the other.

A typical red, yellow, or green LED needs about 10–20 mA (milliamps) of current and "uses up" about 2 V of the supply. Here is how to work out the resistor value:

  1. Start with the supply voltage: 3.3 V
  2. Subtract the LED's forward voltage (roughly 2 V for a standard red LED): 3.3 − 2 = 1.3 V left for the resistor.
  3. Choose a target current — 10 mA (= 0.01 A) is safe and bright enough. Divide: 1.3 V ÷ 0.01 A = 130 Ω.
  4. Round up to the nearest standard resistor value. The closest common value above 130 Ω is 270 Ω or the even safer 330 Ω. Either works perfectly. A 330 Ω resistor is the standard beginner choice.
Tip: When in doubt, use a 330 Ω resistor for any standard LED connected to a 3.3 V GPIO pin. The LED will be slightly less bright than its theoretical maximum, but it will last for years.

Reading Resistor Colour Bands

Resistors are colour-coded. A 330 Ω resistor has four colour bands: Orange – Orange – Brown – Gold. You can also just check the label on its plastic bag if it came in a starter kit — this is the quickest approach.

What Is a Breadboard?

In plain words: A breadboard is a plastic board full of tiny holes. Inside, metal strips connect certain holes together so you can plug in components and wire them up without any soldering. You simply push wires and component legs into the holes, and the metal inside makes the electrical connection.

A standard half-size breadboard is divided like this:

  • Two long rows along the top and bottom edges, labelled + and −, running the full length. These are called power rails — all holes in the same rail are connected together.
  • A large central area with numbered rows (usually 1–30). Each row is split into two halves (a–e on the left, f–j on the right) by a gap down the middle. Every hole in the same half-row (e.g., 1a, 1b, 1c, 1d, 1e) is connected together, but the left half and right half are NOT connected to each other across the gap.

So if you push an LED's leg into hole 10e and a wire into hole 10c, they are connected. But a wire in 10f is not connected to 10e — it is on the other side of the gap.

Project 1 — Blink an LED

This is the "Hello, World!" of physical computing: making an LED flash on and off under the Pi's control. It proves that your software can talk to the real world.

What You Need

  • A Raspberry Pi 5 (already set up and running Raspberry Pi OS)
  • 1 × LED (any colour — red is traditional)
  • 1 × 330 Ω resistor (orange-orange-brown-gold bands)
  • 2 × jumper wires (short wires with push-on connectors — female-to-male: the female end fits a GPIO pin, the male end fits a breadboard hole)
  • 1 × mini breadboard
Careful: Power off your Pi before wiring anything. Shut down from the desktop (click the Raspberry Pi menu > Shutdown), wait for all the green lights to go out, then remove the power lead. Wiring with the power on is the most common way to accidentally damage a GPIO pin. Only plug the power back in once you are confident your wiring is correct.

Wiring Diagram

Step-by-Step Wiring Instructions

Careful: Your Pi must be fully shut down and unplugged from power for all wiring steps. Do not plug it back in until step 8.
  1. Prepare your breadboard. Place the mini breadboard on a flat surface in front of you with the numbered rows visible. You will use rows 5 and 6 in this project — but any two adjacent rows work fine.
  2. Insert the LED. Push the LED's two legs into the breadboard so that the long leg (anode, positive) goes into hole 6e and the short leg (cathode, negative) goes into hole 6f. Because the centre gap separates e from f, each leg is in its own separate electrical group — perfect.
  3. Insert the resistor. Push one leg of the 330 Ω resistor into hole 6b (the same row as the LED's long leg, so they are connected by the breadboard's internal strip). Push the other resistor leg into hole 4b (a different row, which gives you a free connection point for the GPIO wire).
  4. Connect the GPIO wire. Take a female-to-male jumper wire. Push the female end (the socket) firmly onto physical pin 11 on the Pi's GPIO header — that is GPIO 17, left column, sixth pin from the top. Push the male end (the pin) into hole 4a — the same row as the free leg of the resistor.
  5. Connect the GND wire. Take a second female-to-male jumper wire. Push the female end onto physical pin 6 on the GPIO header — that is a GND pin, right column, third from the top. Push the male end into hole 6g — the same row as the LED's short leg on the right-hand side of the breadboard.
  6. Trace the full circuit. Follow the path: GPIO 17 (pin 11) → jumper wire → hole 4a → resistor → hole 6b → LED long leg (6e) → LED short leg (6f) → hole 6g → jumper wire → GND (pin 6). Every step should be connected, with no wires touching each other except where intended.
  7. Check the LED orientation. The long leg must connect toward the GPIO side (via the resistor). The short leg must connect toward GND. Getting this backwards simply means the LED will not light — it will not damage anything. If the LED does not come on, try pulling it out and turning it around.
  8. Only now, plug in your Pi's power lead and let it boot to the desktop.

Writing the Python Program

The Pi's recommended Python library for GPIO is called gpiozero. It comes pre-installed on Raspberry Pi OS Bookworm and uses the lgpio backend on the Pi 5 — so there is nothing to download.

In plain words: A library is a collection of ready-written code that you can use in your own programs. Instead of writing dozens of lines to control a GPIO pin from scratch, you import gpiozero and use its simple commands like LED(17).

Open Thonny (click the Raspberry Pi menu → Programming → Thonny Python IDE). You will see a blank editor area at the top. Type the following program exactly as shown:

from gpiozero import LED
from signal import pause

led = LED(17)
led.blink()
pause()

Line 1 — from gpiozero import LED: The gpiozero library contains ready-made objects for common components. This line pulls in the LED object so we can use it. Think of it as fetching the right tool from a toolbox.

Line 2 — from signal import pause: Brings in a helper called pause() that keeps the program running and waiting. Without it, the program would finish and close in an instant before the LED had a chance to blink.

Line 4 — led = LED(17): Creates a software object representing the LED connected to GPIO pin 17. The number 17 is the GPIO (BCM) number, which corresponds to physical pin 11 on the header.

Line 5 — led.blink(): Tells the LED to start blinking — on for one second, off for one second, repeating forever. You can customise the speed (see the Tip below).

Line 6 — pause(): Keeps the program alive indefinitely so the blinking continues. The program will stay here until you stop it.

Tip: You can control the blink speed by passing arguments to blink(). For example, led.blink(on_time=0.2, off_time=0.8) makes the LED flash quickly (on for 0.2 seconds, off for 0.8 seconds). Try different values and experiment!

Running the Program

  1. In Thonny, click File → Save and save the file as blink.py in your home folder.
  2. Click the green Run button (the triangle ▶) at the top of Thonny, or press F5.
  3. Watch your LED — it should start blinking on and off once per second. If it does not, check your wiring: most likely the LED is backwards (swap it around), or a jumper wire is not pushed in firmly.
  4. To stop the program, click the red Stop button in Thonny (the square ◼). The LED will go off and stay off.
Note: If Thonny shows a pin-factory error such as No module named 'lgpio', the lgpio backend may need to be installed. Open a terminal and run sudo apt install python3-lgpio, then close and reopen Thonny. If you see a permissions error, make sure your user account is in the gpio group (see the section "Checking GPIO Access" later in this chapter).

Congratulations — you have just written a program that controls a physical object in the real world. That is the essence of physical computing.

Project 2 — Read a Push Button

In Project 1, your Pi was the boss — it sent signals out. In Project 2 you flip it around: you will tell the Pi to listen for a signal coming in from a push button you press with your finger. Press the button, the LED comes on. Release it, the LED goes off.

What You Need

  • Everything from Project 1 (LED + 330 Ω resistor + breadboard still wired up)
  • 1 × push button (a small tactile switch — four legs, fits a breadboard)
  • 1 × additional female-to-male jumper wire
Careful: Shut down and unplug your Pi before adding the button wiring.

How a Push Button Works

A small tactile push button has four legs, but electrically it only has two connections: one pair of legs on one side, and one pair on the opposite side. When you press the button, the two sides connect. When you release it, they disconnect again.

To use a button with gpiozero, you connect one side to a GPIO pin and the other side to the 3.3 V power pin. The gpiozero Button object automatically switches on an internal pull-down resistor inside the Pi. This invisible resistor gently pulls the GPIO pin to LOW (0 V) whenever the button is open. When you press the button, 3.3 V flows into the pin and it reads HIGH. You do not need any external resistor for the button — gpiozero handles it all.

In plain words: A pull-down resistor is a resistor connected between a GPIO pin and GND. It ensures the pin reads a definite LOW (0 V) when nothing else is driving it. Without it, the pin would "float" — picking up electrical noise and randomly reading HIGH or LOW. The Pi 5 has these built in, and gpiozero enables them automatically when you create a Button object.

Wiring the Button

Push the button across the centre gap of the breadboard (its legs straddle the gap) in a new area of rows, away from the LED circuit — for example rows 12–13.

  1. Push the button across the breadboard centre gap — legs straddling the gap in rows 12–13 (or any spare rows). This keeps the two sides of the button electrically separate until it is pressed.
  2. Connect the GPIO 27 wire. Take a female-to-male jumper wire. Push the female end onto physical pin 13 on the GPIO header — that is GPIO 27, left column, seventh pin from the top. Push the male end into the breadboard hole on the left side of the button (e.g., row 12, hole b).
  3. Connect the 3.3 V wire. Take another female-to-male jumper wire. Push the female end onto physical pin 1 on the GPIO header — that is the 3.3 V pin, top-left corner of the header. Push the male end into the breadboard hole on the right side of the button (e.g., row 12, hole f — the other side of the centre gap).
  4. That is all for the button. You do not need a GND wire for the button itself — gpiozero enables a built-in pull-down resistor inside the Pi chip, which holds the GPIO 27 pin at LOW when the button is not pressed. The LED circuit from Project 1 stays exactly as it is.
  5. Double-check, then reconnect power and let the Pi boot up.
Note: The left and right sides of a tactile button are defined by looking at it from above. Two legs on one side are always connected together; the other two legs on the opposite side are always connected together. When you press the button, the left pair connects to the right pair. If your button is in a 4-hole square arrangement, left and right are the pairs that face each other across the longer axis.

The Button Program

Open a new file in Thonny (File → New) and type the following:

from gpiozero import LED, Button
from signal import pause

led = LED(17)
button = Button(27)

button.when_pressed = led.on
button.when_released = led.off

pause()

Line 1 — from gpiozero import LED, Button: This time we import two objects from gpiozero: the same LED from Project 1, and a new one called Button.

Line 2 — from signal import pause: Same as before — keeps the program alive so it can keep listening for button events.

Line 4 — led = LED(17): Represents our LED on GPIO 17, exactly as before.

Line 5 — button = Button(27): Represents the push button connected to GPIO 27. By default, gpiozero enables the built-in pull-down resistor, keeping the pin at LOW until the button is pressed — no extra wiring needed.

Line 7 — button.when_pressed = led.on: Tells Python: "Whenever the button is pressed, run led.on()" — which switches the LED on. Notice there are no parentheses after led.on; you are handing the action to the button to call later, not calling it right now.

Line 8 — button.when_released = led.off: Tells Python: "Whenever the button is released, run led.off()" — switching the LED off again.

Line 10 — pause(): Keeps the script running indefinitely so it can respond to button events. Without this, the program would end immediately.

Save this as button_led.py and run it with the green button in Thonny. Now press your physical button — the LED should light up while you hold the button, and go off when you release it.

Tip: You can make the button do anything you like. Instead of controlling an LED, you could make it print a message. Here is how to define your own action for the button:
def button_was_pressed():
    print("Button was pressed!")

button.when_pressed = button_was_pressed

Lines 1–2 — def button_was_pressed(): and print(...): Define a new function called button_was_pressed. The indented print line below it is what happens when the function is called — it displays a message in the Thonny console.

Line 4 — button.when_pressed = button_was_pressed: Hands this new function to the button object, so gpiozero calls it every time the button is pressed. Again, no parentheses — you are pointing at the function, not calling it yet.

Checking GPIO Access in Bookworm

Raspberry Pi OS Bookworm manages GPIO permissions through user groups. Your user account is automatically added to the gpio group during first-time setup, so GPIO programs should work without any changes. If you ever get a "permission denied" error when running GPIO code, open a terminal and check which groups your account belongs to:

groups

groups: Prints all the user groups your account belongs to. Look for gpio in the output. If it is missing, run the command shown below to add yourself, then log out and back in for the change to take effect.

If gpio is not listed, run this to add your account:

sudo usermod -aG gpio $USER

sudo: Runs the command with administrator ("superuser") privileges — required for changing group memberships.

usermod -aG gpio: Modifies a user account (usermod) by appending (-a) them to an additional group (-G gpio). The gpio group is the one that grants access to GPIO pins.

$USER: A built-in variable that automatically expands to your current username, so you do not need to type it yourself.

Note: After running the command above, you must log out and log back in (or reboot the Pi) before the change takes effect. Group memberships are only read when you first log in.

Going Further: Sensors, the Camera, and HATs

Once you are comfortable with an LED and a button, a whole universe of electronics opens up. Here are the most popular next steps for Pi beginners.

Sensors

Sensors let your Pi sense the world around it. They all connect to the GPIO header (or sometimes via USB).

PIR Motion Sensor Example

A PIR (Passive Infrared) sensor detects heat from people and animals moving nearby — it is what triggers security lights. Wire it so its VCC pin goes to a 5 V power pin (most HC-SR501 PIRs need 5 V for their internal electronics), its GND pin to any GND pin, and its OUT signal pin to GPIO 4. Then:

from gpiozero import MotionSensor
from signal import pause

pir = MotionSensor(4)

pir.when_motion = lambda: print("Motion detected!")
pir.when_no_motion = lambda: print("All quiet.")

pause()

Line 1 — from gpiozero import MotionSensor: Imports the ready-made gpiozero object for PIR sensors.

Line 4 — pir = MotionSensor(4): Creates a MotionSensor object listening on GPIO 4 (physical pin 7). gpiozero reads the signal from the sensor and handles all the pin logic internally.

Line 6 — pir.when_motion = lambda: print("Motion detected!"): lambda is a quick way to write a tiny, one-line function without giving it a name. This says: when motion is detected, print a message to the console. You could replace this with any action — flashing an LED, sending an email, starting a video recording.

Line 7 — pir.when_no_motion = lambda: print("All quiet."): The same pattern for when motion stops being detected.

Line 9 — pause(): Keeps the script running indefinitely, waiting and listening for motion events.

The Raspberry Pi Camera Module

The Pi 5 has two dedicated camera connectors (labelled CAM0 and CAM1) — slim ribbon-cable sockets on the board. An official Raspberry Pi Camera Module v2 or v3 plugs in with a flat ribbon cable. Once connected, you can take photos and record video in Python using the picamera2 library, which is pre-installed on Bookworm.

Note: On the Pi 5 with Bookworm, the camera works automatically as soon as the ribbon cable is connected — there is no enable toggle needed in settings. The picamera2 library uses the modern libcamera stack. You can test that the camera is detected by opening a terminal and running rpicam-hello: a preview window should appear for a few seconds.
from picamera2 import Picamera2

cam = Picamera2()
cam.start()
cam.capture_file("photo.jpg")
cam.stop()

Line 1 — from picamera2 import Picamera2: Imports the Picamera2 library that comes with Bookworm for controlling the camera hardware.

Line 3 — cam = Picamera2(): Creates a camera control object. If no camera is detected, an error will appear here.

Line 4 — cam.start(): Powers up the camera sensor and starts the image pipeline. There may be a brief warm-up pause.

Line 5 — cam.capture_file("photo.jpg"): Takes a single photograph and saves it as photo.jpg in the current folder (usually your home folder when run from Thonny).

Line 6 — cam.stop(): Powers the camera down neatly after the photo is taken.

Tip: The Camera Module v3 has a motorised autofocus lens — it focuses automatically. The v2 has a fixed-focus lens. Both work with the same picamera2 code. Find the saved photo.jpg in the Files app (in your home folder) to view it.

HATs — Hardware Attached on Top

In plain words: A HAT (Hardware Attached on Top) is an add-on board that plugs directly onto the full 40-pin GPIO header, sitting on top of the Pi like a hat on a head. HATs follow a standard design that allows the Pi to automatically identify what is connected.

Popular HATs for beginners include:

  • Sense HAT — contains an 8×8 LED matrix display, temperature, humidity, pressure and compass sensors, and a joystick, all in one board. A fantastic all-in-one learning tool — and the same board used on the International Space Station.
  • Explorer HAT / Motor HAT — controls DC motors and stepper motors, great for robots.
  • Pimoroni Unicorn HAT — a grid of colourful LEDs for light shows and animations.
  • NVMe Base HAT — adds an M.2 NVMe SSD via the Pi 5's PCIe connector (this one goes underneath the Pi, not on the GPIO header).

Each HAT comes with its own Python library and installation instructions. Most install with a single sudo apt install command or a one-line installer script from the manufacturer's website.

Note: The Pi 5 uses a new version of the HAT standard called HAT+. Most older HATs designed for the Pi 4 still fit physically, but always check the manufacturer's compatibility notes for the Pi 5 before buying.

GPIO Safety Checklist

Before every session with the GPIO header, run through this quick checklist:

  • Power off before wiring. Shut down the Pi fully (menu → Shutdown), wait for the green activity light to go out, then unplug the power lead. Only connect or change wiring with no power.
  • Never feed 5 V into a GPIO input pin. The Pi 5 GPIO is 3.3 V logic only — 5 V will destroy the pin instantly and permanently.
  • Never short two pins together. Letting two metal pins touch via a stray wire or misplaced component can instantly damage the Pi. Always check your wiring carefully before powering on.
  • Always use a current-limiting resistor with LEDs. A bare LED with no resistor will burn out in seconds — and may take the GPIO pin with it.
  • GPIO pins supply only a small current. Each GPIO pin can safely supply up to about 8–16 mA, and the total for all pins combined is limited. Do not try to power a motor, relay, or high-current buzzer directly from a GPIO pin — use a driver board or transistor circuit instead.
  • Handle with care and avoid static. Avoid putting your fingers on the Pi's circuit board components while the power is on. Before handling the bare board, touch a grounded metal object (such as a radiator) to discharge any static electricity from your body.

Key terms

  • GPIO - General-Purpose Input/Output; the 40 programmable pins on the Pi that can communicate with electronic components.
  • Header - The double row of 40 metal pins along the top edge of the Pi board.
  • BCM number - The GPIO pin numbering system used in software (e.g., GPIO 17), as opposed to the physical position on the header (e.g., physical pin 11).
  • Voltage (V) - Electrical "pressure", measured in Volts. The Pi's GPIO pins output 3.3 V.
  • Current (A / mA) - The flow of electricity, measured in Amperes or milliamps. Each GPIO pin can safely supply around 8–16 mA.
  • Resistance (Ω) - Opposition to current flow, measured in Ohms. A series resistor protects components from receiving too much current.
  • Ohm's Law - The rule that Current = Voltage ÷ Resistance, used to calculate safe resistor values for LEDs and other components.
  • LED - Light-Emitting Diode; a small efficient lamp that lights up when current flows through it in the correct direction.
  • Anode - The positive leg of an LED (the longer one); current enters here.
  • Cathode - The negative leg of an LED (the shorter one); current exits here toward GND.
  • Series resistor - A resistor placed in-line with an LED to limit current to a safe level. 330 Ω is the standard beginner choice for a 3.3 V GPIO.
  • Breadboard - A reusable plastic prototyping board with internal metal strips connecting rows of holes, allowing components to be connected without soldering.
  • Pull-down resistor - A resistor connected between a GPIO input pin and GND that keeps the pin reading LOW when nothing else is driving it. The Pi 5 has these built in; gpiozero enables them automatically for buttons.
  • gpiozero - A beginner-friendly Python library pre-installed on Raspberry Pi OS Bookworm that makes controlling GPIO components very simple. On Pi 5 it uses the lgpio backend.
  • lgpio - The low-level GPIO library used as the backend by gpiozero on the Raspberry Pi 5 / Bookworm. Pre-installed; install with sudo apt install python3-lgpio if ever missing.
  • PIR sensor - Passive Infrared sensor; detects movement by sensing changes in infrared (heat) radiation from warm bodies.
  • picamera2 - The Python library pre-installed on Bookworm for controlling the Raspberry Pi Camera Module using the libcamera stack.
  • HAT - Hardware Attached on Top; an add-on board that plugs onto the 40-pin GPIO header and follows a standard that lets the Pi identify it automatically.

Chapter summary

  • The 40-pin GPIO header is the Pi's gateway to the physical world, providing 3.3 V and 5 V power pins, ground pins, and up to 26 programmable GPIO pins.
  • GPIO pins operate at 3.3 V logic only — feeding 5 V into them will permanently damage the Pi 5.
  • Always power off and unplug the Pi completely before connecting or changing any wiring.
  • An LED always needs a series resistor (330 Ω is the safe standard for a 3.3 V GPIO) to prevent it burning out.
  • A breadboard lets you prototype circuits quickly without soldering, using internal metal strip connections — left and right halves of each row are separated by a centre gap.
  • Software uses the BCM (GPIO) number, not the physical pin position; LED(17) controls GPIO 17 = physical pin 11.
  • The gpiozero library on Bookworm uses the lgpio backend on Pi 5; LED(17) and Button(27) work out of the box with no extra configuration.
  • Project 1 demonstrated output: blinking an LED by driving GPIO 17 HIGH and LOW in a repeating pattern with led.blink().
  • Project 2 demonstrated input: reading a push button on GPIO 27 and using gpiozero's built-in pull-down resistor so that button.when_pressed and button.when_released trigger actions automatically.
  • The correct button circuit with gpiozero is: one button leg to GPIO pin, other leg to 3.3 V — just two wires, no external resistor required.
  • Sensors (PIR, DHT22, ultrasonic), the Camera Module (use picamera2 on Bookworm), and HAT add-on boards are natural next steps that all build on the same GPIO foundation.
